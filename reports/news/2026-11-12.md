<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
The Bluetooth SIG adopted High Data Throughput (HDT) on 4 Nov 2026, raising the maximum Bluetooth LE bit rate to 7.5 Mbps, about 4x the 2 Mbps of today's LE 2M PHY. HDT defines five rate tiers: HDT2, HDT3 and HDT4 are mandatory for any controller that claims HDT, HDT6 is optional, and HDT7.5 is optional on top of HDT6. Coverage reports HDT as an optional feature of Bluetooth Core 7.0, so a "Bluetooth 7" badge does not guarantee it, and first products are expected in 2027 with lossless audio as the flagship use case. 7.5 Mbps is a raw PHY rate; application throughput after headers, ACKs and connection-event gaps will be lower. Why it matters: BLE stops being a "small packets only" link, which changes the trade-off between BLE and Wi-Fi for audio, sensor-array and image-transfer products like Life_logger and the P2CAM camera.

<b>1. Post: Capability unlock — BLE at 7.5 Mbps</b>
Bluetooth LE just got a 7.5 Mbps mode, but only 4 Mbps of it is guaranteed.

On 4 Nov the Bluetooth SIG adopted High Data Throughput (HDT). It defines five rates: 2, 3 and 4 Mbps are mandatory for any HDT controller, 6 Mbps is optional, and 7.5 Mbps is optional on top of that.

The engineering detail: the headline is a PHY bit rate. Your real throughput is set by connection interval, packet length, ACKs and how long the controller keeps the event open. On today's 2M PHY most firmware never reaches the ceiling anyway, so HDT rewards teams who already tune their link layer.

Products are expected in 2027, with lossless audio first.

Which of your BLE designs would actually be limited by the PHY rather than by the connection parameters?

<b>2. Post: Tooling unlock — the vendor plugs into your AI agent</b>

STM32Cube for Agentic AI connects coding agents to ST-maintained documentation and examples, and adds a first review pass on what the agent generates. A separate assistant, STM32 Sidekick, answers questions with citations back to the docs.

Why this matters: generic models are weakest exactly where embedded work is hardest, in part-specific errata, clock trees and peripheral quirks. Grounding the agent in the vendor's own material attacks the right problem. ST's comparison is its own, though, so independent results will tell us how much it helps.

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — MRAM replaces flash</b>
Renesas just moved a mainstream industrial MCU family from flash to MRAM.

The RX74M and RX74N are the first RX parts on a 22 nm process. They pair a 600 MHz core with 1 MB of embedded MRAM, 256 KB of zero-wait TCM and 1.5 MB of SRAM.

The physics is the point. Embedded flash gets hard to scale at advanced nodes, while MRAM stores bits magnetically and fits better with logic processes. You also get a memory that is written very differently from flash, which can change how you think about logging, configuration storage and OTA staging.

The 600 MHz part is listed at $10.74 in 1k quantities, per CNX.

If your code storage no longer needs page erases, what would you redesign first: your bootloader or your data logger?

<b>4. Post: Integration leap — RISC-V enters the AURIX world</b>
Automotive firmware for Infineon's first RISC-V AURIX can be written today, before the first silicon exists.

On 4 Nov Infineon announced AURIX RC1, a RISC-V family for zone, body, chassis and battery-management control. Hardware samples are targeted for the first half of 2027, but a virtual prototype is available now, bundled with a Synopsys virtualizer, HighTec's compiler and Lauterbach TRACE32.

The interesting part is not the ISA. It is that safety-grade automotive control, one of the most conservative corners of embedded, is moving to an open ISA and starting software bring-up on a model rather than a board.

<b>5. Post: Security wake-up — the LAN is not a trust boundary</b>
A CVSS 8.8 bug that requires an attacker to already be on your LAN.

Zyxel's CVE-2026-76955 is a classic buffer overflow (CWE-120) in the zdp_wtp binary on certain access points, FWA7 devices and security routers. A LAN-side attacker can use it to escalate privileges. The fix is a firmware update; the advisory gives no workaround.

The lesson for firmware teams: "only reachable from the local network" is not a mitigation. Guest Wi-Fi, a compromised IoT device or a laptop with malware all put an attacker on the LAN. Local discovery and management daemons deserve the same input validation and fuzzing as anything facing the internet.

When did you last fuzz the local service your device exposes for setup?
