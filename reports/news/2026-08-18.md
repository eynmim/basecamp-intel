<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Security researchers at Binarly disclosed six vulnerabilities in U-Boot's FIT (Flattened Image Tree) image-verification code, the most severe being CVE-2026-46728: a signature-verification bypass caused by omitting the hashed-nodes field, letting a crafted firmware image skip signature checks entirely on U-Boot versions before 2026.04. The other five (BRLY-2026-038 through 042) cover out-of-bounds reads, a null-pointer dereference, unbounded recursion, and unvalidated externally-stored firmware data, all reachable during the boot-time image-parsing path. Because most of the vulnerable code has existed since U-Boot 2013.07, it potentially touches more than 50 releases and every vendor who forked that code into their own bootloader, and exploitation happens before the OS loads, so there's nothing for a running-system antivirus or EDR to catch. Why it matters: U-Boot is the default bootloader on the overwhelming majority of embedded-Linux boards Ali is likely to touch in coursework or thesis work, and a bypassed signature check means a "secure boot" chain that isn't actually verifying anything.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — a bootloader bug that's been hiding since 2013</b>
Your "secure boot" chain can pass every check and still not be verifying anything.

Researchers just disclosed CVE-2026-46728: a bypass in U-Boot's firmware signature verification caused by simply omitting one field, the hashed-nodes field, from a crafted image. Do that, and the bootloader skips signature checking entirely. Five companion bugs in the same code path add out-of-bounds reads, a null-pointer crash, and unbounded recursion that can exhaust the boot stack.

The part that stings: most of this code has existed since U-Boot 2013.07. Thirteen years, 50+ releases, and every vendor who forked that parsing logic into their own bootloader inherited it silently. Exploitation happens before the OS loads, so there's no running-system defense that ever sees it.

If you're shipping a board with U-Boot, do you actually know which release it's on, or are you trusting the "secure boot" label on the datasheet?

<b>2. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — the 2.4GHz band got a scheduling rewrite, not a power bump</b>
Bluetooth 6.0 didn't make radios faster. It made them better at taking turns.

The spec's core change is coexistence scheduling: refined timing logic for sharing the same crowded 2.4GHz spectrum with Wi-Fi 6/7, Thread, and Zigbee, cutting collisions and retransmissions rather than adding bandwidth or range. Bluetooth 6.1 then layered Randomized RPA address rotation on top for privacy and a small power win.

The insight that's easy to miss: in a band this congested, the bottleneck usually isn't transmit power or receiver sensitivity, it's how gracefully your radio yields the channel to everyone else's. A scheduling fix can matter more for real-world link stability than a raw PHY improvement would.

Next time a wireless design underperforms in the lab and then falls apart on a crowded office floor, is it your radio's problem, or the neighborhood's?

<b>3. Post: Capability unlock — one PMIC just replaced your indoor and outdoor solar circuits</b>
Indoor light and outdoor sunlight look nothing alike to a photovoltaic cell, which is why harvesting both used to mean two separate PMIC designs.

e-peas' AEM15820 collapses that into one chip: it auto-switches between a low-power and a high-power boost converter, does cold-start from just 5μW at 275mV, and handles MPPT across the full range from dim indoor light to direct sun. It just landed on Mouser's shelves, so it's not a paper part anymore.

What actually unlocks here isn't the efficiency number, it's the design freedom: a product that moves between an indoor shelf and an outdoor mount no longer needs a PMIC swap or a dual-PMIC board layout, just different lighting conditions hitting the same input pin.

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — the visa quota isn't the path that matters for engineers</b>
Italy just locked in 164,850 non-EU work visas for 2026 under its new three-year Decreto Flussi plan. If you're an engineer, that number is almost a distraction.

The quota system covers seasonal work, self-employment, and general non-seasonal roles through a capped, competitive "click day" process. But highly skilled roles in engineering, IT, and healthcare have a separate, uncapped route: the EU Blue Card, which sits entirely outside the Decreto Flussi quota for positions meeting a roughly €38,000 gross salary threshold.

The engineering takeaway: for technical roles, the headline immigration-quota number and the actual path you'd use to get hired are two different systems, and conflating them leads to worrying about the wrong bottleneck.
