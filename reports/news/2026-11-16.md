<b>2. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — Bluetooth 7.0 is not 7.5 Mbps</b>
A "Bluetooth 7.0" sticker will not tell you whether a device runs at 7.5 Mbps.

The Bluetooth SIG adopted High Data Throughput in Core 7.0, raising the LE PHY rate from 2 Mbps to up to 7.5 Mbps. But HDT is optional. A product can qualify as 7.0 without it.

The engineering reason it cannot be retrofitted: HDT is a new physical layer, not a protocol feature. The modulation lives in the radio, so both ends need new silicon. No firmware update moves a 2 Mbps radio to 7.5 Mbps.

HDT is also not a codec. Lossless audio will be a separate specification on top of it, which the SIG expects in 2027.

When you write a product spec, do you list the core version, or the features you actually depend on?

https://www.bluetooth.com/specifications/specs/high-data-throughput/

<b>3. Post: Security wake-up — the update endpoint is the attack surface</b>
The most dangerous endpoint on a device is often the one that installs firmware.

CVE-2026-8065 in Hitachi Energy's RTU500 CMU firmware is classed CWE-306, missing authentication for a critical function: the firmware-update endpoint does not require a login, so an attacker on the network can upload arbitrary firmware. It is scored 9.1, and the affected firmware is end-of-life.

The engineering point: authenticating the request and verifying the image are two separate controls. A signed-image check stops modified firmware. An authenticated endpoint stops anyone on the network from even starting an update. Industrial gear tends to live for decades, so both must be in place on day one, because many units will never get a patch.

Does your bootloader verify signatures, and does your update API check who is calling it?

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — the CRA gets its vocabulary</b>
The EU's first Cyber Resilience Act standards are due this week, and the first one contains no requirements at all.

EN 40000-1-1 is a vocabulary. EN 40000-1-2 covers principles, risk management and the product lifecycle. Both cleared the CEN-CENELEC vote and are scheduled for publication around 17 November.

The nuance: the vocabulary standard is marked "no citation expected", so it will never give presumption of conformity. The standard closest to daily firmware work, EN 40000-1-3 on secure development and vulnerability handling, is still a draft.

Why read a vocabulary standard anyway? Because from now on, auditors, customers and job descriptions in the EU will describe your OTA, logging and patch process in these terms.
