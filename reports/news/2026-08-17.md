<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — Zephyr didn't disclose four CVEs this week, it disclosed ten</b>
Everyone's talking about the four-CVE Zephyr batch. Nobody's talking about the other six that landed two days earlier.

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — long-range Wi-Fi just became a part you can actually buy</b>
Wi-Fi HaLow has been a spec on paper since 2016. This month it became a module with a part number, an FCC cert, and a supply chain.

Quectel's FGH200M, built on Morse Micro's MM8108 SoC, just passed FCC/IC/CE/RCM certification for mass production — an 11x10x2mm module in the 850-950MHz band with 1/2/4/8MHz channel options, aimed at visual sensing, autonomous mobile robots, and industrial IoT.

What's actually new here isn't the radio technology, it's the design decision it unlocks: LoRaWAN gives you range but not bandwidth, Wi-Fi gives you bandwidth but not range, and HaLow has always promised to sit between them — but promising doesn't help you when you can't get a certified module with a real lead time. <b>3. Post: Integration leap — one RISC-V chip just absorbed Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth, Thread, Zigbee, and Gigabit Ethernet</b>
Five radios and a wired MAC, one die, one part number.

Espressif's ESP32-S31 entered mass production this month: a dual-core 320MHz RISC-V SoC combining Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth 5.4 (LE Audio and Classic), 802.15.4 for Zigbee and Thread, Matter support over Wi-Fi or Thread, and a Gigabit Ethernet MAC — with one core carrying a 128-bit SIMD datapath for compute-heavy tasks.

The interesting part isn't any single radio, it's that this makes "smart home hub" or "protocol bridge" a single-chip category instead of a design that used to need an SoC plus a separate Ethernet PHY plus a Thread border-router radio stitched together. It's also RISC-V, not Xtensa, so it's a genuinely new toolchain target rather than a drop-in S3 upgrade.

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — an open-source framework just showed you what "compliant" actually costs in code</b>
RED's cybersecurity rules have been legally enforceable in the EU for a year. This July, we finally got to see what compliance costs in actual commits.

ESPHome — a widely-used open-source ESP32/nRF52 firmware framework — shipped its first EN 18031-targeted security infrastructure in its 2026.7.0 release: NVS encryption, OTA downgrade protection, and a transport-agnostic provisioning component. That's roughly a year after RED cybersecurity enforcement went live, and weeks before the EU CRA's next harmonised-standards deadline lands Aug 30, followed by mandatory vulnerability reporting on Sep 11.

What this makes concrete is the gap between "the law is in force" and "the tooling catches up" — if a major open-source framework needed a year to ship baseline compliance features, plenty of production firmware out there hasn't started yet.

<b>5. Post: Tooling unlock — the industry just agreed on how to measure a microcontroller's AI efficiency</b>
"How efficient is this chip at inference" used to mean whatever benchmark the vendor's marketing team picked. That's no longer true.

MLPerf Tiny v1.4 results are out, built over roughly 18 months by more than 50 organizations — Harvard, Google, ST, Qualcomm, Renesas, Infineon, Silicon Labs, and CERN among them — around a standardized methodology that treats energy, not just latency, as a first-class metric for sub-1mW MCU inference.

The unlock isn't the benchmark numbers themselves, it's that TinyML deployment decisions can now be made on a common yardstick instead of vendor-selected demos — the same shift that happened to server-side ML once MLPerf's main track matured.
