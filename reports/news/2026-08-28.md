<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Espressif shipped ESP-IDF v6.1 on Aug 27, and it's not a routine point release. It adds preview support for the ESP32-S31, a new unencrypted-PSRAM capability for carving out DMA regions that skip flash encryption, and Wi-Fi additions including OWE-only SoftAP and experimental Wi-Fi Aware Pairing for iOS — but it also carries real breaking changes: MQTT moved out of the core into the component manager, several GPIO APIs were renamed, the LCD driver was unified around FourCC pixel formats, the PSA key-storage format changed (requiring re-import of existing keys), and Secure Boot dropped 192-bit curve support while disabling ECDSA V2 on the chips already flagged in July. The same release also quietly patches a JPEG-decoder security advisory (GHSA-v6r2-f6p2-88cj). Why it matters: this is the SDK every ESP32-S3 project you touch depends on, and the breaking changes are exactly the kind that silently fail a build or a secure-boot flash months later if you don't budget migration time now.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — malware just learned your car has a computer worth hijacking</b>
Your car's infotainment screen just became a more interesting attack target than your router.

Kaspersky found the first malware built specifically for automotive head units: a component called "JarService" that hijacks TWCore, the legitimate over-the-air update app on DoFun-brand Android car units, and quietly turns the vehicle into a residential-proxy node for ad and click fraud. Code and infrastructure overlap point to MoYu Group, the same actor behind the BADBOX botnet that's been living in cheap Android TV boxes and set-top boxes for years.

The pattern worth noticing: BADBOX didn't build new malware for cars, it ported an existing supply-chain playbook — compromise the OTA app, not the OS — onto a device class nobody was watching. Any embedded system with an internet-connected update mechanism and a trusted system app is the next candidate.

If your product has an OTA updater, would you notice if it started doing something else on the side?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — a 5G-class IoT protocol just went fully open source</b>
DECT NR+ has been an interesting spec on paper for a while. This week it became something you can actually build with.

The Opener Initiative published an Apache-2.0 reference implementation of the DECT-2020 NR MAC and upper-layer stack, running on Zephyr on Nordic's nRF91 silicon. DECT NR+ is license-exempt spectrum (1880-1930 MHz in the EU, soon extending down to 3.8-4.2 GHz), needs no base station infrastructure, and supports point-to-point, star, and mesh topologies while carrying a 5G IMT-2020 qualification.

What makes this a genuine unlock rather than a spec announcement: it's the first open reference stack for the protocol. Every prior DECT NR+ implementation lived behind a vendor NDA. Now it's a Zephyr board file and a west build away.

Anyone evaluating it as a LoRaWAN or NB-IoT alternative — what's stopping you from just trying it?

<b>3. Post: Tooling unlock — Espressif shipped a release that will break your build on purpose</b>
ESP-IDF v6.1 is the rare SDK release that breaks things deliberately, and that's actually the point.

MQTT moved out of ESP-IDF's core and into the component manager. Several GPIO APIs got renamed. The PSA key-storage format changed, meaning existing provisioned keys need re-importing before you flash v6.1 firmware. Secure Boot dropped 192-bit curve support entirely. None of this is an accident — it's Espressif clearing technical debt before it compounds.

The engineering lesson: a "breaking changes" section in release notes is not a formality to skim. The PSA key-storage change specifically will pass CI and then fail in the field on a device that still has old-format keys provisioned — the kind of bug that only shows up on hardware you can't easily re-flash.

When was the last time you actually read a migration guide before merging an SDK bump?

<b>4. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — a chip that only draws 200 nanoamps is doing your MCU's job for it</b>
200 nanoamps. That's the idle current of a chip designed to make your main MCU irrelevant most of the time.

Nanopower Semiconductor's new nPZ2100 sits between your sensors and your MCU, drawing 200 nA idle and 1 µA while actively polling. It manages up to six peripherals over I2C/SPI on its own, using an integrated SRAM, ADC, and timing block to decide locally whether anything worth waking the host for actually happened.

The architectural shift underneath the spec sheet: this isn't a lower-power MCU, it's an admission that even an ultra-low-power MCU still burns too much just deciding "nothing happened yet." Push that decision into a companion IC an order of magnitude leaner, and the host MCU's duty cycle stops being the constraint.

For a battery-or-harvester-powered design, would you rather have a leaner MCU or a smarter gatekeeper in front of it?

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — the EU's big CRA deadline this week isn't actually a deadline</b>
Aug 30 has been circled on a lot of compliance calendars as an EU Cyber Resilience Act deadline. It arrives today with nothing legally binding attached to it.

Aug 30 was CEN/CENELEC/ETSI's original target to deliver the harmonized standards manufacturers need for CRA's "presumption of conformity" route. The European Commission quietly pushed the real drafting deadlines to Oct 31 and Dec 31 back in July, and as of today, zero CRA harmonized standards have been cited in the Official Journal — for any product category.

The lesson for anyone tracking compliance dates: a standardization deadline and a legal obligation are not the same thing, and only one of them actually changes what you have to do Monday morning. The date that does bind you is Sept 11, when mandatory 24-hour vulnerability and incident reporting goes live via ENISA's platform.

Are you tracking CRA by the headline deadlines, or by which ones actually carry an obligation?
