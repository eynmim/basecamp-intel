<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
CERT Polska's continued audit of the MikroTik "MikroTrick" disclosure has added two more CVEs to what started as a two-bug SSH story. CVE-2026-67277 lets a "related" btest connection get accepted before its primary session finishes authenticating, and CVE-2026-67278 is a malformed RSA/PKCS#1 v1.5 signature-validation flaw in X.509 handling that lets an attacker controlling or redirecting an outbound RouterOS TLS connection forge a trusted intermediate certificate using nothing but a root CA's own public key. That's a different failure class than yesterday's SSH auth bypass — it's a break in the TLS trust chain itself, not just the login path. MikroTik has now patched all four CVEs across RouterOS 7.24.2, 7.23.4 and 6.49.21, but exploitation of the original SSH pair began a full day before the first patched builds shipped. Why it matters: a disclosure that looked like "one router botnet" a day ago has grown into a four-CVE chain spanning auth bypass, session confusion and certificate forgery — a reminder that one systemic flaw found in a widely deployed embedded network stack usually isn't the only one.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — a router's real weak point wasn't its front door</b>
A router's SSH login wasn't the real problem. Its TLS trust chain was.

MikroTik's "MikroTrick" disclosure just grew from two CVEs to four. CERT Polska's continued audit surfaced CVE-2026-67278: a malformed RSA/PKCS#1 v1.5 signature bug in X.509 validation that lets an attacker who controls or redirects an outbound RouterOS TLS connection forge a trusted intermediate certificate — using nothing but a root CA's own public key.

That's a different failure mode than the earlier SSH auth bypass. It's not "get in" — it's "make the device trust something it shouldn't while it thinks it's talking to a legitimate server." MikroTik has now patched all four CVEs, but exploitation of the original SSH pair started a full day before patches shipped.

If you maintain embedded TLS stacks: how carefully do you validate signature algorithms during X.509 chain building, versus just checking expiry and revocation?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — a companion chip that lets your MCU sleep through six sensors</b>
A companion chip that lets your MCU sleep through six sensors talking at once, on 200 nanoamps.

Nanopower Semiconductor's new nPZ2100 sits between your sensors and your MCU, autonomously polling up to six I²C/SPI devices, checking thresholds and managing timing — all while drawing just 200nA at 3V in idle. The host processor wakes only when something actually happens.

The genuinely new part isn't "sensor hub" — those exist. It's the current budget: 200nA idle, plus four 1mA peripheral switches and a dedicated 10mA host switch, eliminates idle leakage across the whole sensor chain, not just the MCU. For a coin-cell or energy-harvested design, that turns the MCU from the power bottleneck into just one part of a bottleneck-free chain.

If you've built a sensor-polling layer in firmware just to keep the MCU asleep longer, would offloading it to silicon like this change your architecture?

<b>3. Post: Tooling unlock — TFLite Micro has a real MCU-class competitor now</b>

That conversion step is historically where accuracy quietly degrades or ops silently fail to map — cutting it out is a real developer-experience win, not just a smaller footprint number.

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — the EU's 24-hour disclosure clock is about to get tested</b>
The EU's new 24-hour vulnerability disclosure clock is about to get its first real stress test.

The Cyber Resilience Act's exploited-vulnerability reporting duty went live on September 11 — manufacturers now have 24 hours to notify ENISA once they know a vulnerability in their product is being actively exploited. MikroTik's evolving MikroTrick disclosure, now four CVEs deep, is exactly the messy, multi-stage kind of incident this rule was written for.

The hard part isn't the first report. It's what happens when a second, third and fourth related CVE surface days or weeks later from the same root cause — the CRA's text doesn't clearly separate "new incident" from "escalation of one already disclosed," and that ambiguity is going to matter the first time a regulator actually tests it.

If you ship a connected product into the EU: does your incident-response process already assume a disclosure will evolve over weeks, not resolve in one report?
