A nation-state espionage crew and a ransomware gang used the exact same vulnerability. Not similar bugs — the same one.

Cisco Talos this week confirmed three separate intrusion clusters exploiting CVE-2026-20079, an auth bypass in Secure Firewall Management Center's web interface that hands out root OS access with no credentials. A management-plane auth bypass is equally useful to an operator planting long-term persistence and to a crew that just wants to encrypt your file server by Friday. If you're triaging "how likely is this to be exploited," you're already asking the wrong question once the interface is reachable.

<b>2. Post: Tooling unlock — the "pick an open RTOS" question just got a real third answer</b>
For years, "open-source RTOS" meant FreeRTOS or, more recently, Zephyr. That's no longer the whole list.

Apache NuttX shipped 13.0.1 on September 15, a stabilization patch following the breaking-change 13.0 major release. With Arm having sunset Mbed OS in July, NuttX is now one of just three actively-maintained open RTOS projects with real traction, alongside Zephyr and FreeRTOS — and it's the one most engineers, myself included, have spent the least time actually using.

What makes NuttX interesting architecturally is that it targets POSIX compliance more aggressively than Zephyr or FreeRTOS do, so code written against a NuttX board often ports to Linux userspace with far fewer changes than a typical RTOS task would need.

Is NuttX's POSIX-first approach worth trading against Zephyr's larger driver ecosystem for your next project?

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — sleep current and shelf-life current are not the same number</b>
Every low-power MCU spec sheet has a sleep-current figure. Almost none separately spec what happens to a battery-installed device sitting in a warehouse for six months.

Nordic's nRF54LC10A, a compact BLE/Thread/Zigbee/Matter SoC, distinguishes an active hibernation mode under 50 nanoamps from its roughly 0.5 microamp runtime sleep current. That's more than a 10x gap between "the device is deployed and idle" and "the device is inventory."

The insight: a power budget built only around active-sleep current will systematically overestimate shelf life, because storage and shipping put a device into a different, lower-power state than any duty-cycled sensing loop ever reaches. If your BOM includes a coin cell that has to survive a supply chain before it survives a customer, hibernation current is the number that actually matters first.

Do you know your own board's hibernation current, or just its active-sleep number?

<b>5. Post: Capability unlock — Zigbee just got a certified path to sub-GHz range</b>
Zigbee has spent two decades boxed into 2.4GHz, the same crowded band as Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. That constraint just got a certified way out.

The Connectivity Standards Alliance opened product certification for Suzi, a sub-GHz extension to Zigbee 4.0 operating around 800MHz in Europe and 900MHz in North America. Device makers now have a formal interoperability, security and performance certification route for sub-GHz Zigbee hardware, rather than a one-off proprietary radio design.

What's notable isn't the frequency shift by itself — sub-GHz range is old news from LoRaWAN and Wi-Fi HaLow. It's that Zigbee gets to keep its existing mesh stack, tooling and device ecosystem while adding the range and wall-penetration of a lower band, instead of forcing a rewrite onto a completely different protocol.

Would a certified sub-GHz Zigbee option change your radio choice for a range-constrained project, versus reaching for LoRaWAN by default?
