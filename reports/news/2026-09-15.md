<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
The EU Data Act's Article 3(1) "access by design" obligation kicked in on September 12 for every connected product placed on the EU market from that date forward. It requires manufacturers to design the product itself, not just a cloud backend, so the data it generates is available to the user by default, in a structured, machine-readable format, without a separate request process. That reaches vehicles, industrial and agricultural machinery, IoT sensors, monitoring equipment and smart-home devices — any embedded product with a data-generating sensor and a network stack. For firmware teams this is an architecture decision, not a paperwork one: it typically means a local API or export interface has to be designed into the product from day one, alongside the CRA's security-reporting duties that went live the day before. Why it matters: two major EU obligations for connected devices landed within 24 hours of each other this month, and both push compliance requirements down into the firmware architecture stage rather than leaving them as an afterthought.

<b>1. Post: Regulatory shift — the EU just made "your data, by default" a firmware requirement</b>
Since September 12, a connected product sold in the EU has to hand you your own data without you asking for it.

That's the EU Data Act's Article 3(1) "access by design" obligation. It doesn't just apply to the cloud dashboard — it applies to the product itself. Vehicles, industrial machinery, IoT sensors, smart-home devices: if it generates data and has a network stack, it now needs a structured, machine-readable, locally-accessible way to expose that data by default.

The part that lands on firmware engineers, not lawyers: this has to be an architecture decision made at design time, not a compliance patch bolted on before shipping. It landed one day after the Cyber Resilience Act's 24-hour vulnerability-reporting duty went live — two EU obligations, one week, both pushing requirements down into how you architect the product.

If you're designing a connected product for the EU market, is "local data export" already in your block diagram, or still a TODO?

<b>2. Post: Security wake-up — a router botnet you can spot with one grep</b>
No password. No exploit chain most people would call sophisticated. Just a malformed SSH key and an argument-parsing bug, and attackers get root on your router.

That's MikroTrick: CERT Polska found attackers chaining CVE-2026-67276 (RouterOS accepts a forged RSA key without needing the real private key) with CVE-2026-86060 (an SSH login argument bug that rewrites the trusted policy mask) to take over internet-exposed RouterOS devices with zero credentials. It's under active exploitation right now.

The detail I like: there's a clean compromise indicator. Attackers create a login user with a name ending in "-2" as a side effect of the exploit chain. One grep against your user list tells you if you've already been hit — no log correlation, no threat-intel feed required.

If you run RouterOS anywhere — home lab included — have you checked your user list for a "-2" account yet?

<b>3. Post: Tooling unlock — TinyML models can now hand off to a real accelerator</b>
"TinyML" has meant "whatever the MCU's CPU can chew through" for years. That assumption just got a documented exit ramp.

A pull request merged into TFLite Micro on September 14 adds an export path to Coral Edge TPU hardware via LiteRT. Instead of a model living entirely inside your MCU's CPU budget, you can now target an actual NPU accelerator through a supported, maintained toolchain path — not a hand-rolled integration.

Why this matters more than it sounds: the hard part of edge AI was never "can the model run" — it's "can I move it between compute targets without rewriting the deployment pipeline." A first-class export path is what turns "accelerator support" from a one-off hack into something you can plan a product architecture around.

<b>4. Post: Integration leap — one automotive chip, four workloads that used to need four chips</b>
Real-time motor control with sub-10-millisecond deadlines. AI inference. Audio processing. Low-power background logic. Infineon just argued all four belong on the same RISC-V zone controller.

At Hot Chips 2026, Infineon laid out the case for RISC-V in automotive "zonal" architecture — the industry's move away from one-domain-per-chip toward zone controllers that consolidate multiple, wildly different workload classes onto a single part. Their design adds an MPU island next to the real-time MCU core, running a POSIX adaptive stack for non-critical workloads so the safety-critical core stays untouched.

The insight that stands out: E-motor control needs interrupt latency in the tens of nanoseconds; a POSIX service layer tolerates multiple seconds. Fitting both on one die means the architecture, not just the process node, is what has to scale — you can't just clock a bigger core faster and call it done.

Where do you draw the line between "consolidate onto one chip" and "keep it separate for safety margin"?

<b>5. Post: Capability unlock — a battery IC that watches 2µA while balancing 70mA</b>
Multi-cell battery monitoring used to cost you standby power just to keep watching. STMicro's new IC barely sips it.

The L9962 integrates cell monitoring, active balancing, current sensing and protection into one part, with a 12-bit ADC and 70mA of active cell-balancing current — while its low-power standby mode draws as little as 2µA.

What's actually new here isn't any single spec — it's the combination. A part that can aggressively balance cells when active but nearly disappear from the power budget when idle is exactly the profile multi-cell packs in e-bikes, power tools and industrial battery systems need, because those packs spend most of their life idle, not balancing.

If you've designed around discrete balancing + monitoring before, would a 2µA-standby integrated part change your BOM decision?
