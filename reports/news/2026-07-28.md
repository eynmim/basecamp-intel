<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
CISA, the FBI, NSA, EPA, DOE, Treasury, and US Cyber Command updated a joint advisory on July 22 confirming Iranian-affiliated actors have expanded a PLC-exploitation campaign beyond Rockwell Automation to also hit Schneider Electric and Siemens controllers — CompactLogix/Micro850, BMX P34/Modicon M340, and S7-1200 families — across US water, energy, and government-facility networks. The attackers deployed no malware at all: they reached internet-exposed engineering interfaces using the vendors' own legitimate configuration software (Studio 5000 Logix Designer, EcoStruxure Control Expert, TIA Portal) over leased overseas infrastructure, then modified or deleted logic inside add-on instructions and altered HMI/SCADA display data — in at least one case disabling shutdown and alarm logic so operators watched normal-looking readings while the process drifted unsafe. CISA's rewritten guidance is blunt: disconnect PLCs and other OT assets from the public internet now, and audit reusable code modules for unauthorized changes. Why it matters: this is a live demonstration that "no malware found" is not the same as "no compromise" — if an engineering workstation has write access to production logic over any reachable network path, an attacker doesn't need an exploit, just your own tools and your own credentials path.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the hack that used no malware at all</b>
Zero malware. Zero exploits. The attackers just logged in with the same software the engineers use every day.

CISA's updated advisory this week confirms Iranian-affiliated actors have expanded a PLC campaign from Rockwell to Schneider Electric and Siemens controllers across US water, energy, and government facilities. They reached exposed engineering interfaces with Studio 5000, EcoStruxure Control Expert, and TIA Portal — legitimate configuration tools — then quietly edited PLC logic and spoofed HMI/SCADA readings so operators saw "normal" while the process drifted unsafe.

The uncomfortable engineering lesson: "no malware found" during incident response tells you nothing if the attacker's access path was your own tooling over a reachable network. Segmentation and access control on engineering workstations matter as much as patching the PLC firmware itself.

Does your OT network actually enforce that engineering software can only reach controllers from an isolated workstation, or does convenience win most days?

<b>2. Post: Regulatory shift — the EU just handed out the compliance answer key</b>
67 worked examples. Flowcharts. Actual use cases instead of legal prose. That's not how EU tech regulation guidance usually reads.

On July 27 the European Commission published practical, non-binding guidance for the Cyber Resilience Act — clarifying what counts as in-scope, what a "substantial modification" is, and how to meet Article 14's incident-reporting duties. It's aimed squarely at SMEs and microenterprises who don't have compliance departments.

The part worth noting: the guidance arrives with the mandatory 24-hour vulnerability/incident-reporting obligation now under 50 days away (September 11, 2026). For anyone shipping a connected device into the EU — including a student project heading toward a startup — that reporting pipeline needs to exist before the deadline, not after an incident forces the question.

<b>3. Post: Tooling unlock — RISC-V finally gets real instruction trace</b>
Cortex-M developers have had cycle-accurate instruction trace for years. RISC-V debugging has mostly meant breakpoints and printf.

SEGGER just added Andes Trace decoding to its mainstream J-Link and J-Trace probes — a hybrid trace format combining the open N-Trace standard with an efficient encoder, meaning developers get streaming instruction trace on RISC-V targets without sourcing vendor-specific trace hardware.

The gap this closes is bigger than it sounds: trace is often the only realistic way to debug a timing bug or a rare RTOS race condition that vanishes the moment you attach a conventional debugger. RISC-V shipping in mainstream MCU families without equivalent trace tooling has been a real productivity tax — this narrows it.

If you're evaluating a RISC-V MCU for a new project, does its trace/debug story actually match what you're used to on Cortex-M?

<b>5. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — ranging accuracy without new hardware</b>
Centimeter-level distance measurement, over a radio that's already in every phone and every BLE car key. No UWB chip required.

Bluetooth Core 6.3 refines Channel Sounding with Inline Phase Correction Transfer — letting the reflecting device apply phase pre-compensation directly in hardware instead of round-tripping raw phase data for host-side processing — plus per-PHY round-trip-time accuracy reporting for better multi-PHY ranging decisions.

The engineering angle that matters: this isn't just a speed optimization. Precise, low-latency distance bounding is exactly the property that breaks the relay attack against keyless-entry systems, where an attacker extends the range of a real key's signal to unlock a car it was never near. Folding that into the existing BLE radio, rather than requiring separate UWB silicon, is what makes it viable for cost-sensitive designs.

Would you trust BLE-based distance bounding for a security-critical design yet, or still reach for dedicated UWB?
