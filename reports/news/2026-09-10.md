<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
CERT Polska disclosed "MikroTrick" on September 5: a chained exploit combining CVE-2026-67276 (CVSS 9.2) and CVE-2026-86060 that lets an attacker take full administrative control of a MikroTik RouterOS device over SSH with no valid credentials at all. The root cause is embarrassingly simple — RouterOS checks an incoming SSH public key's type and modulus against the authorized-keys list but never checks the exponent, so an attacker who knows an authorized RSA modulus can forge a key with exponent 1, sign a valid-looking authentication request, and open a shell as that user; a second bug then lets a crafted username escalate that session straight to admin. ShadowServer counted roughly 122,500 RouterOS devices with SSH exposed to the internet as of September 5, and by September 7 multiple outlets confirmed active in-the-wild exploitation predates the public disclosure by several days. Fixed trains are out (7.24.2 stable, 7.23.4 long-term, 6.49.21 v6 long-term). Why it matters: this is a full remote pre-auth device takeover in a widely deployed embedded router OS, caused by an authentication check that looked correct but wasn't — the kind of subtle crypto-comparison bug worth internalizing regardless of vendor.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the auth check that forgot to check</b>
A router's SSH key check that verifies two out of three parts of the key is, mathematically, no check at all.

CERT Polska disclosed "MikroTrick" on September 5: RouterOS compares an incoming SSH public key's type and modulus against its authorized-keys list, but never checks the exponent. An attacker who knows an authorized user's RSA modulus can forge a new key pair with exponent 1, produce a signature RouterOS accepts as valid, and open a command shell as that user — no password, no private key, no valid credential of any kind. A second bug then escalates that session to full admin. ShadowServer counted roughly 122,500 internet-exposed RouterOS devices as of the disclosure date, and exploitation in the wild reportedly predates the public writeup by several days.

The lesson generalizes past this one router OS: "checking most of a cryptographic value" is functionally the same as not checking it. If you've ever written or reviewed a key-comparison routine — for BLE pairing, for secure boot, for anything — the question worth asking is whether every field that matters actually gets compared, not just the ones that were easy to compare.

Patched trains are out (7.24.2/7.23.4/6.49.21). Have you audited a key-comparison routine recently, or just trusted it worked because the tests passed?

<b>2. Post: Regulatory shift — one day until "report it or answer for it"</b>
Starting tomorrow, if your connected product has a security hole under active attack, EU law gives you 24 hours to tell a regulator — and the clock doesn't care how big your team is.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act's Article 14 reporting duty becomes enforceable September 11: manufacturers of products with digital elements sold into the EU must report actively exploited vulnerabilities to ENISA and their national CSIRT within 24 hours of becoming aware, followed by a 72-hour technical notification and a 14-day final report. Critically, this covers products already on the market — not just things shipped after the deadline.

The part that doesn't get said enough: the CRA's definition of "manufacturer" isn't scoped to large companies. A university side project, a thesis prototype someone downloads and flashes, a small-batch Kickstarter board — if it has a radio and a CPU and reaches an EU customer, the same 24-hour clock applies to a one-person team as to a multinational.

Do you know, right now, which national CSIRT you'd file through if you had to?

<b>3. Post: Capability unlock — Zephyr just got a new home on ultra-low-power silicon</b>

Ambiq announced expanded Zephyr Project support for its Apollo330 Plus and Apollo510 Lite SoC series on September 8 — six variants spanning wearables, hearables, AR devices, and industrial sensors, all now first-class Zephyr targets rather than requiring Ambiq's own SDK from scratch.

The interesting part isn't the chip, it's the porting cost this removes. That's a meaningfully lower bar to actually benchmark a design against, instead of defaulting to whatever MCU the last project used out of habit.

Worth a weekend if a sensor-node project has been assuming its current MCU is the low-power ceiling without ever checking.

<b>4. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — Europe tapes out its own edge-AI answer</b>
Most edge-AI chip stories are about importing silicon designed somewhere else. This one is about not doing that.

Published September 8, the EU Horizon project CONVOLVE reported taping out a chip built by co-designing the AI algorithm and the hardware together, rather than the usual approach of squeezing a generic model onto generic silicon. The result runs selected inference workloads locally, at lower energy cost, explicitly positioned as a European-built alternative in a field currently dominated by non-EU silicon.

The "so what" here is architectural, not political: algorithm-hardware co-design is a genuinely different discipline from picking an off-the-shelf NPU and quantizing a model to fit it. When the instruction set, the memory hierarchy, and the model architecture are all decided by the same team at the same time, you get efficiency gains that bolting an accelerator onto an existing core structurally can't reach.

If TinyML work has meant "shrink a model to fit the chip," this is a reminder there's a second lever: designing the chip around the model instead.

<b>5. Post: Price-floor shift — the battery-free tag just went retail-scale</b>
Ninety million. That's how many battery-free IoT tags one retailer alone is targeting in circulation by the end of this year.

Wiliot's ambient-RF-powered "IoT Pixels" are being deployed by Walmart for pallet-level tracking, with a stated goal of 90 million tags by end of 2026. These tags carry no battery at all — they harvest enough energy from ambient RF to power a sensor read and a transmission, and nothing more.

What makes this worth noting isn't the tag, it's the threshold it crosses: energy harvesting has spent years as a lab demo and a niche industrial sensor story. A named retailer committing to tens of millions of units is a different category — it means the harvesting circuit, the power budget, and the duty cycle have all been proven reliable enough for a supply chain that can't tolerate silent failures.

If energy harvesting has felt like a "someday" technology for a project idea, this is the signal that "someday" arrived for at least one use case already.
