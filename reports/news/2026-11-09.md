<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Nordic announced the nRF71 series on 3 Nov: a single-chip family combining Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth LE, Thread and an optional on-device NPU. The first parts are the nRF7120 (dual-band Wi-Fi 6) and nRF7120E (adds 6 GHz and the Axon NPU). Nordic claims up to 20x lower Wi-Fi standby power than its nRF70 series, and the Wi-Fi radio can receive at the same time as the 2.4 GHz multiprotocol radio, so a device can hold a cloud link while listening for BLE or Thread. It uses the same nRF Connect SDK (Zephyr-based) as nRF54 and nRF70, and coverage describes it as customer sampling with no pricing yet. Why it matters: it is the clearest sign yet that battery-powered Wi-Fi plus edge inference is becoming a mainstream SoC category, directly in the space where ESP32-S3 sits today.

<b>1. Post: Integration leap — Wi-Fi and BLE listening at once</b>
Battery Wi-Fi devices usually have to choose: stay on the cloud link or listen for local radios.

Nordic's new nRF71 series claims to remove that trade-off. Its Wi-Fi radio can receive at the same time as the 2.4 GHz multiprotocol radio, so BLE or Thread traffic doesn't have to wait for Wi-Fi to sleep.

For firmware, that changes the scheduling problem. Less time-slicing and coexistence logic means fewer edge cases around missed advertisements and reconnect storms.

Nordic also claims up to 20x lower Wi-Fi standby power than nRF70. I'd want the test conditions before believing any battery-life number.

<b>2. Post: Tooling unlock — ExecuTorch on Cortex-M</b>
A vendor benchmark just put TFLite Micro on the back foot.

Arm measured ExecuTorch's Cortex-M backend against TFLM on an Alif Ensemble E8. ExecuTorch had lower latency on 3 of 4 models and a smaller flash footprint on all 4.

The interesting part is the design: CMSIS-NN kernels where they exist, portable kernels as fallback. You get acceleration without losing operator coverage.

It's Arm's own test, so I'd treat it as a reason to re-measure, not a verdict. Flash is often the first wall on a microcontroller, so the footprint number is the one I'd check first.

<b>3. Post: Security wake-up — signed images that aren't safe</b>
A signature check is only as strong as the code that verifies it.

Binarly reported six flaws in U-Boot's FIT signature verification. Two are remote code execution, and the report says they break secure boot across more than 50 firmware releases.

The lesson for me: secure boot is not a checkbox. The parser sits before the trust anchor does its job, so bugs in image parsing can undercut the whole chain.

Some older devices may never get patches. If you ship U-Boot, check your version and your update path.

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — the CRA clock</b>
The date firmware teams should circle is 11 December 2027.

According to an industry webinar from September, that's when the Cyber Resilience Act becomes fully applicable, and when the RED cybersecurity delegated act is planned for repeal.

One detail that surprised me: EN 18031 gives presumption of conformity for RED, but not for the CRA. Passing today's radio-equipment tests won't automatically cover tomorrow's rules.

For engineers, that means secure boot, signed OTA and vulnerability handling stop being nice-to-have. Final adoption status is worth checking on EUR-Lex.

<b>5. Post: Capability unlock — NPU inside a Wi-Fi SoC</b>
Edge AI is quietly moving into the radio chip itself.

Nordic's nRF7120E pairs tri-band Wi-Fi 6E with the Axon NPU, the same accelerator it introduced in the nRF54LM20B earlier this year. The plain nRF7120 has no NPU.

That split matters when picking parts: inference-ready silicon with Wi-Fi in one package means one less chip on the BOM and one less interface to power-manage.

It's customer sampling for now, with no pricing published. I'd wait for the datasheet before designing around it.
