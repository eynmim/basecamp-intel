<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
A newly tracked stack-based buffer overflow in BlueZ — the Bluetooth protocol stack underneath most embedded-Linux BLE gateways and Linux-hosted central devices — lets any nearby device trigger memory corruption in bluetoothd with a single crafted Extended Inquiry Response (EIR) packet sent during ordinary discovery. CVE-2026-80186 (CVSS 7.6) needs no pairing, no authentication, and no user interaction: the target only has to be scanning for nearby devices, which is the default behavior of almost every BLE central. A successful hit reliably crashes the Bluetooth daemon and, depending on the target's stack layout and mitigations, opens a path to arbitrary code execution. It was assigned on August 25, and open-source patch-tracking roundups published as recently as September 1 still list it unresolved on a meaningful share of deployed BlueZ builds heading into September. Why it matters: if an embedded-Linux project uses BlueZ as its BLE central instead of a firmware-only stack like NimBLE, this is a bug it inherits passively the moment discovery starts — before anything has even connected.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — one packet, no pairing required</b>
A single Bluetooth packet sent during ordinary device discovery can crash — or potentially hijack — the daemon almost every embedded-Linux BLE gateway depends on.

CVE-2026-80186 (CVSS 7.6) is a stack-based buffer overflow in BlueZ, triggered when a nearby device sends a crafted Extended Inquiry Response packet during standard BLE discovery. No pairing, no authentication, no user action — the target just has to be scanning, which is the default state of almost any BLE central.

The part worth sitting with: this fires before your application logic ever sees a connection. If your project's BLE central role runs on BlueZ instead of a firmware-only stack, this bug lives upstream of every access-control decision you've made.

Do you know whether your gateway's BLE stack is BlueZ or something firmware-native — and does that choice show up in your threat model?

<b>3. Post: Regulatory shift — a question most teams haven't answered yet</b>
Nobody has told most embedded teams shipping into the EU which government office to call when their firmware gets hacked.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act's Article 14 makes vulnerability and incident reporting mandatory on Sept 11 — 24 hours for an early warning, 72 for a full notification. Seven days out, ENISA's filing portal still has no published access URL, but the reporting duty starts on schedule regardless.

The one piece of prep that doesn't depend on the portal: identifying your CSIRT-coordinator Member State, or your Article 18 authorised representative if you ship from outside the EU. That's a paperwork question you can answer this week, before the clock is running on a real incident.

Do you know which CSIRT you'd be filing with on Sept 11?

<b>5. Post: Tooling unlock — a smaller RTOS just entered lockdown mode</b>
There's a POSIX-compliant RTOS with a smaller footprint than Zephyr, and its next release just went bugfix-only.

Apache NuttX's release manager cut the releases/13.1 branch on Sept 1, opening a backport window that runs through Sept 25 — after that, RC0 ships and the release vote starts. It's a routine milestone, but it's also a reminder that Zephyr isn't the only actively-maintained RTOS option worth benchmarking against your board-support requirements.

The tradeoff worth actually testing, not assuming: Zephyr's larger driver ecosystem and vendor support versus NuttX's tighter POSIX compliance and smaller memory footprint — the answer usually depends on which BSP you're targeting, not which project has more GitHub stars.
