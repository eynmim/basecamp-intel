<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
CISA published ICS advisory ICSA-26-267-01 on September 24 for the Botslab G980H dash camera, disclosing two chained flaws: CVE-2026-85496 (CWE-330, CVSS 8.8), where the firmware generates session identifiers from a small sequential value space instead of a cryptographically secure random source, and CVE-2026-77967 (CWE-294), an improper-authentication bug in the same session-handling path. Together they let an unauthenticated attacker with adjacent-network access — anyone who can reach the device's Wi-Fi or LAN segment, no credentials needed — guess or replay a valid session ID and hijack an active viewing session, pulling live video or stored recordings straight off the device. Botslab has not responded to CISA's outreach to coordinate a fix, so there is no patch and no ETA. Why it matters: predictable session tokens are a textbook embedded mistake — the same shortcut a time-pressured firmware team takes when a proper CSPRNG feels like overkill for "just a session ID," and it's exactly the kind of shortcut worth auditing out of any of your own connected-camera or IoT-device projects before it ships.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — a dashcam that hands out sessions like ticket numbers</b>
A dash camera's session IDs turned out to be about as random as a deli-counter ticket number.

CISA's September 24 advisory for the Botslab G980H dashcam (CVE-2026-85496, CVSS 8.8) describes firmware that generates session identifiers from a small sequential value space instead of a cryptographically secure random source. Paired with a companion authentication weakness (CVE-2026-77967), anyone on the same network segment can guess or replay a valid session and pull live video or stored recordings — no login required. Botslab hasn't responded to CISA's outreach, so there's no patch.

The uncomfortable part isn't the bug class, it's how ordinary it is. "Just generate a session ID" is exactly the kind of code a time-pressured firmware team writes without reaching for a CSPRNG, because a counter feels like it does the job.

Have you actually checked what's generating your own device's session tokens, or just assumed it's "random enough"?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — patch diffing that used to take a week now takes a weekend</b>
A vendor shipped a silent security fix. Days later, the exact vulnerability it patched was public — reconstructed by an LLM agent reading the diff.

CERT Polska's September 22 technical write-up on "MikroTrick," the actively-exploited MikroTik RouterOS SSH auth-bypass chain, spends less time on the bug itself and more on how it got fully understood so fast: LLM-assisted patch diffing reconstructed the vulnerable code paths from MikroTik's official fix within days of release.

That collapses an assumption a lot of vendors still quietly rely on — that shipping a fix without a detailed advisory buys weeks of quiet remediation time before attackers reverse-engineer it. If an LLM agent can do that reconstruction over a weekend, "we'll patch silently and explain later" stops being a viable disclosure strategy.

If you shipped a security fix tomorrow with zero advisory detail, how many days would you actually have before the bug was public anyway?

<b>3. Post: Integration leap — one credential, three radios, your pick which one shows up</b>
Your phone unlocking a door over NFC, BLE, or UWB — same credential, same standard, vendor doesn't matter.

At embedded world North America (Sept 22–24), Nuki Home Solutions demoed a full Aliro-compliant digital lock built on STMicroelectronics silicon and software. Aliro, the Connectivity Standards Alliance's 1.0 access-control spec released in February, defines a single trust framework and credential format that runs interchangeably over NFC, Bluetooth LE, or ultra-wideband.

The interesting part is the "interchangeably." Access-control hardware has historically locked you into one radio and one vendor's ecosystem per product line. Aliro treats NFC/BLE/UWB as three transports for the same protocol instead of three separate products — the same kind of abstraction Matter did for smart-home device control, just one layer lower, at the radio.

Would you rather standardize on one radio for an access-control project, or build once and let Aliro pick the transport?

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — Italy just wrote its biggest work-visa number ever</b>
497,550. That's how many non-EU work permits Italy just committed to issuing over the next three years — its largest quota ever.

The newly approved Decreto Flussi 2026-2028 sets annual quotas across seasonal, non-seasonal subordinate, and self-employed categories, with roughly 230,550 slots reserved for non-seasonal subordinate and self-employed workers spanning transport, construction, manufacturing and beyond. Separately, and outside this quota entirely, the EU Blue Card continues to offer non-EU tech and engineering talent a faster (30-90 day) processing path with strong intra-EU mobility rights.

The detail worth noticing: two parallel systems now exist side by side for the same pool of skilled non-EU workers, and they're not interchangeable — one is capped and clickday-driven, the other isn't capped by this decree at all. Which one applies to you depends entirely on job category and employer sponsorship route, decided well before any visa application gets filed.

If you're a non-EU engineer building a career in the EU, do you know which of these two paths your target employer would actually sponsor you through?

<b>5. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — the package disappears into the wafer</b>
The most expensive part of a power chip's footprint used to be the package around the silicon. onsemi just deleted that layer.

onsemi's Embedded Power Platform, introduced September 16, uses the silicon wafer itself as the foundation of the package rather than mounting bare die into a separate package structure afterward — the company claims 3-5x higher power density than current solutions as a result.

Power density in a design has always been fought on two fronts: shrinking the silicon, and shrinking everything wrapped around it. Most power-IC roadmaps only ever attack the first front. Folding the package into the wafer process attacks the second front directly, and it's the kind of architectural move that usually forces a rethink of board layout and thermal design more than it forces a rethink of the circuit itself.

Would a 3-5x power-density jump change your enclosure or battery sizing more than your actual power-tree design?
