<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Infineon has added the PSC3M6 and PSC3P6 to its PSOC Control C3 Main Line: 180 MHz Cortex-M33 parts, with the M6 aimed at driving up to four BLDC motors, sensored or sensorless. The notable part is secure boot: firmware is verified with SHA-512 plus LMS (Leighton-Micali hash-based signatures), which Infineon aligns with the CNSA 2.0 suite, so post-quantum signature checking arrives on a mainstream motor-control MCU. Sources agree the parts are available now, but disagree on PSA Level 3 status (enablement versus certified), so check Infineon's product page before relying on a certificate. Why it matters: hash-based boot signatures are moving from research into ordinary control MCUs, which will shape long-lived product designs under the EU Cyber Resilience Act.

<b>1. Post: Capability unlock — post-quantum boot on a motor MCU</b>
Post-quantum signatures just showed up on a chip that spins four BLDC motors.

Infineon's PSC3M6 and PSC3P6 are 180 MHz Cortex-M33 parts, and their secure boot verifies firmware with SHA-512 plus LMS, aligned with the CNSA 2.0 suite.

What I find interesting is LMS is hash-based: security rests on the hash function rather than on elliptic curves. For products that will be in the field for a decade, that is a boot-chain choice you make now, not later.

Would you pick a hash-based boot signature for a product that ships in 2027?

<b>2. Post: Tooling unlock — C17 becomes Zephyr's floor</b>
A quiet line in the Zephyr 4.4 notes: C17 is now the default minimum C standard.

The same release moved to Zephyr SDK 1.0, with an upgraded GNU toolchain and experimental Clang/LLVM support.

For firmware teams that means a toolchain bump can quietly change what compiles. I would rebuild one real project against the new SDK before touching a product branch.

When did you last rebuild your firmware with a different compiler, just to see what breaks?
