<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Starting tomorrow, September 12, the EU Data Act's "access by design" obligation becomes enforceable: every newly placed connected product sold into the EU must be built so its operational data and metadata are accessible, secure, and provided in a structured, machine-readable format by default — not only on request. It lands exactly one day after the Cyber Resilience Act's Article 14 mandatory 24-hour vulnerability-reporting duty went live, meaning two separate, major EU regulations now apply back-to-back to anything with a CPU and a radio sold into Europe. Neither obligation carves out an exception for small teams or single-person shops — a university prototype or small-batch board reaching an EU customer inherits the same duties as a multinational. The design duty is limited to "where relevant and technically feasible," but it still pushes data-access architecture into the schematic stage rather than an afterthought. Why it matters: for anyone building connected hardware for the EU market, product architecture now has two new legal constraints baked in from day one, and ignorance of either is not a defense.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the filesystem nobody audits</b>
Seven new CVEs just landed in a filesystem library so old and "solved" that almost nobody thinks to audit it anymore — and it's sitting inside ESP-IDF, STM32Cube, Zephyr, MicroPython, and RT-Thread right now.

runZero disclosed seven bugs in FatFs, the FAT/exFAT driver bundled into most embedded SDKs: an integer overflow in FAT32 mounting that produces a false file size, long-filename buffers that overflow fixed-size fields in downstream code, unsigned-subtraction wraparound that risks silent data corruption, and an unbounded GPT partition-scan loop that hangs the mount. Attack surfaces include USB, SD cards, and malicious firmware update files.

The part that should worry you more than the bugs themselves: the maintainer stayed silent even after coordination through JPCERT/CC. Only one of seven fixes landed upstream — everything else depends on each vendor patching downstream on their own schedule, or not.

Have you actually checked which FatFs version your last firmware build links against?

<b>3. Post: Capability unlock — FreeRTOS stops assuming one core</b>
FreeRTOS just stopped assuming every Cortex-M part only has one core to run on.

Kernel V11.3.1, released in August, adds official Symmetric Multiprocessing support to Armv8-M ports across GCC, ArmClang, and IAR — per-core critical nesting state, spinlocks, inter-core yield and wakeup via SEV/WFE, and synchronized dual-core bring-up, with single-core builds left completely unaffected.

Dual-core Cortex-M55/M85-class silicon has been shipping for a while now, but until this release, running FreeRTOS across both cores meant either vendor-specific patches or writing your own synchronization primitives from scratch. This closes that gap with a portable, upstream implementation instead of a fork you have to maintain forever.

Even if your current board is single-core, it's worth reading the SMP porting notes before your next design — the API surface you'll need is already there.

<b>4. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — 200 nanoamps to keep six sensors alive</b>
200 nanoamps. That's the idle current of a chip that manages six peripherals and decides, on its own, when your actual processor is allowed to wake up.

Nanopower's new nPZ2100 companion IC autonomously handles up to six I2C/SPI peripherals while drawing 200 nA idle and 1 µA while polling — keeping the host MCU fully powered down until there's genuinely something for it to do. Engineering samples ship this month.

This is a different lever than the usual "pick a lower sleep-current MCU" approach to low-power design. It moves the "who's awake right now" decision entirely out of the expensive processor and into a dedicated, purpose-built sequencer — so the MCU only spins up for real work instead of periodically waking just to check whether anything happened.

If periodic polling was the biggest current draw in your last low-power design, this architecture pattern — not a lower-power MCU — might be the bigger win.

<b>5. Post: Integration leap — LoRaWAN finally automates the boring part</b>
Getting a LoRaWAN device onto a network for the first time has quietly been one of the most manual, unglamorous parts of any IoT rollout — someone physically keying in join keys or scanning a QR code, per device, every time.

On August 4 the LoRa Alliance published two new specifications, TS014 and TS018, along with a TR016 guidance document, together enabling automated device set-up and extending LoRaWAN's connectivity reach. The explicit goal is removing manual provisioning as a deployment bottleneck.

Standardizing zero-touch provisioning at the alliance level — instead of every vendor building its own proprietary onboarding flow — is what actually makes a thousand-node rollout feasible for a small team, instead of something that needs a dedicated systems integrator just to get devices talking.
