<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
In nine days — September 11, 2026 — Article 14 of the EU Cyber Resilience Act goes live: any manufacturer placing a product with digital elements on the EU market must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents through ENISA's new Single Reporting Platform, on a staged clock of 24 hours for an early warning, 72 hours for a full notification, and 14 days for a final report. ENISA has been quietly building the plumbing all August — its SRP registration and notification guides were updated August 3, and the platform-interface guide followed August 14, with a testing window expected to run right up to go-live. This is the first CRA deadline with real teeth: it lands more than a year before the December 2027 full-compliance date, and it applies the moment a device ships, not just to new designs. Unlike the "secure by design" harmonised standards (still delayed to October/December, as flagged in this digest yesterday), Article 14 reporting has a fixed date, a named platform, and no grace period. Why it matters: any embedded team shipping into the EU — including a small shop or a student project that scales into a commercial product — needs a working incident-reporting process in nine days, not a roadmap for one.

<b>1. Post: Regulatory shift — nine days to build a reporting pipeline</b>
Nine days from now, "we'll report it eventually" stops being a legal option for anyone shipping a connected device into the EU.

September 11, 2026 is when Article 14 of the Cyber Resilience Act goes live: manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents through ENISA's new Single Reporting Platform — 24 hours for an early warning, 72 hours for a full notification, 14 days for a final report.

What's easy to miss: this deadline has nothing to do with the "secure by design" harmonised standards, which just slipped again to October and December. Article 14 is a fixed date with a named platform and no grace period, and it applies to products already on the market — not just new designs starting today.

If you maintain any firmware repo that could plausibly become a product, a documented disclosure process isn't optional anymore. It's due next Friday.

<b>2. Post: Security wake-up — a stack sentinel that writes past the wall it's guarding</b>
A stack-overflow guard just became the thing that overflows the stack.

FreeRTOS-Kernel's TrustZone ports (ARMv8-M) let a Non-Secure task request a secure execution context with a stack size of its choosing. CVE-2026-77236, disclosed Aug 21, found that SecureContext_AllocateContext() never validated the minimum size — request an undersized stack, and the allocator's own overflow-detection sentinel gets written past the buffer it was supposed to protect, corrupting Secure-heap metadata next door.

The irony is the point: the safety mechanism was the attack surface. It only bites TrustZone configurations, but that's exactly the deployment pattern people reach for when they want hardware-enforced separation between "normal" code and secrets.

Fixed in V11.3.1. If you've ever split a project into Secure/Non-Secure partitions expecting the boundary to hold on its own, this is worth a second look.

https://github.com/FreeRTOS/FreeRTOS-Kernel/security/advisories/GHSA-vq2f-9qj3-jj2m

<b>3. Post: Price-floor shift — an $18 board that beats a Cortex-M4 on CoreMark/MHz</b>
A $17.99 RISC-V dev board just posted a better CoreMark/MHz score than a typical Arm Cortex-M4 — and it comes with Ethernet and USB 2.0 High-Speed built in.

WCH's CH32V407/467, announced Aug 13, pairs a 200MHz Qingke V3V core with RVV vector extensions to hit 4.11 CoreMark/MHz. It integrates a 10/100 Mbps Ethernet MAC+PHY, a 480Mbps USB 2.0 host PHY, and — on the CH32V467 — up to 8MB of on-chip PSRAM for display or HMI work.

What used to require a separate Ethernet PHY, a USB PHY, and external RAM chips is now one part, at a price point where the whole dev board costs less than a single discrete Ethernet PHY did a few years ago. That's not just cheaper — it removes layout and BOM decisions that used to force a bigger board.

Worth a look if your next project needs networked I/O without the usual chip count.

<b>4. Post: Tooling unlock — one platform instead of 27 national inboxes</b>
Compliance reporting across the EU used to mean figuring out which national authority to email. As of this month, it means an API.

ENISA's Single Reporting Platform, the mechanism behind the Sept 11 CRA vulnerability-reporting deadline, replaces ad hoc per-country contact with one submission addressed to your main-establishment CSIRT, automatically mirrored to ENISA. The registration and notification guides were updated Aug 3, and the platform-interface guide followed Aug 14 — real documentation for a real system, not a policy paper.

For engineering teams, that's the difference between "legal handles this somehow" and something you can actually wire a monitoring pipeline into: a structured endpoint with a defined schema and a staged clock (24h / 72h / 14 days) instead of a fax number.

If your team ships into the EU, this is worth reading before the deadline, not after an incident.

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — a 500,000-visa plan with an exemption that matters more than the number</b>
The headline number is 500,000 work visas. The number that actually matters for engineers is zero — as in, zero quota limit.

Italy's newly approved Decreto Flussi 2026–2028 sets three-year non-seasonal work-visa quotas starting at 164,850 for 2026 and rising annually. But EU Blue Card holders and other highly-skilled categories — engineering roles above roughly €38,000 gross — sit entirely outside that quota system, processed year-round instead of competing in a capped clickday window.

That distinction is easy to miss if you only read the headline figure: for a specific profession like embedded engineering, the relevant policy isn't the quota decree at all, it's the exemption that routes around it.

If you're mapping out an EU job search timeline, know which door you're actually walking through.
