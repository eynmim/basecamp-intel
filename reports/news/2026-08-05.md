<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
CISA disclosed ICSA-26-216-01 on August 4: the Acrisure KARR Security System and SWDS dealer-installed automotive anti-theft units use a single shared Bluetooth authentication key across every affected device. Researchers reverse-engineered the key straight out of the official KARR mobile app, then built a proof-of-concept Android app that impersonates a legitimate owner's commands — unlocking doors, sounding the alarm, flashing lights, or immobilizing the engine from up to five yards away, on any of roughly 2.2 million vehicles. Making it worse, dormant units keep broadcasting Bluetooth while the car is running and for up to ten minutes after shutdown, widening the attack window well past "car is parked and locked." Acrisure shipped a firmware fix on July 20, but CISA's advisory this week is the first wide public accounting of the flaw's scale. Why it matters: this is the textbook failure mode of pairing/bonding done wrong — a single static key baked into every unit instead of per-device provisioning — the exact class of mistake worth double-checking in any BLE-paired product design.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — one key, 2.2 million cars</b>
A car alarm company shipped the exact same secret key to every single unit it sold. All 2.2 million of them.

CISA's ICSA-26-216-01 (Aug 4) describes how researchers pulled a shared Bluetooth authentication key straight out of the official KARR mobile app, then built a proof-of-concept Android app that impersonates the owner — unlocking doors, sounding the alarm, or immobilizing the engine from five yards away. Dormant units keep broadcasting Bluetooth for up to ten minutes after the car shuts off, widening the window further.

The interesting part isn't that Bluetooth got broken — it's that nothing needed breaking. A shared key instead of per-device provisioning means "extract it once" equals "own the whole fleet." That's a pairing/bonding design choice, not a crypto flaw.

If you're shipping a BLE-paired product, is your provisioning actually per-device, or did convenience win during bring-up?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — the trick was where the model lives, not how big it is</b>
9 tokens per second, 28.9 million parameters, one $8 microcontroller. The unlock wasn't a smaller model — it was moving the biggest part of it somewhere slower.

Developer slvDev got this LLM generating short stories on an ESP32-S3 by keeping its ~25-million-row embedding table in flash instead of SRAM, streaming only the ~450 bytes each token actually needs. It's the same Per-Layer Embeddings approach Google uses in Gemma 3n, just landed on a part that costs less than lunch.

The insight worth keeping: SRAM budget stops being the hard ceiling on model size once you accept flash-latency reads for the parts you touch sparsely. Dense compute still wants SRAM; sparse lookup tables don't.

Where else in an embedded design are we defaulting to "keep it in RAM" when the access pattern would tolerate flash?

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — the CRA's first real deadline isn't in 2027, it's this month</b>
Everyone frames the Cyber Resilience Act as a "December 2027" problem. For one category of products, the clock runs out in 25 days.

The European Commission published practical CRA compliance guidance on July 27, and horizontal ("Type A") products with digital elements must be compliant by August 30, 2026 — well ahead of both the full-application deadline and the September 11 start of mandatory vulnerability and incident reporting.

The useful habit here isn't memorizing every CRA date — it's knowing that compliance deadlines are staggered by product category, so "I have until 2027" is true for some products and false for others. Anyone shipping into the EU market needs to know which bucket their product falls into, not just the headline year.
