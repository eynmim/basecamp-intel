The EU Cyber Resilience Act's widely-cited "Aug 30 deadline" for Type A harmonized standards is not actually landing today. In early July, the European Commission quietly published a draft amendment to standardisation mandate M/606 that pushes the 2026 drafting deadlines back two months: the horizontal Type A and vulnerability-management Type B standards move from Aug 30 to Oct 31, 2026, and the product-specific Type C standards from Oct 30 to Dec 31, 2026. Why it matters: if you're tracking CRA deadlines for an EU embedded career, the standards timeline and the regulation's compliance timeline are two different clocks, and conflating them (as much of the trade press has) will make you look behind rather than ahead in an interview.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — one missing bounds check turns a network packet into a function call</b>
CVSS 8.8. The root cause is one array index that nobody checked.

Zephyr's virtio driver reads a descriptor-chain ID straight off the device's "used ring" and uses it, unchecked, as an index into an array of callback function pointers. A malicious virtio backend just has to hand back an out-of-range ID, and the driver reads whatever heap memory sits past the array — then calls it as a function.

The generalizable lesson isn't "validate untrusted input," which everyone already nods along to. It's specifically about untrusted values used as array indices into anything containing function pointers or codepointers — that's a different, sharper risk category than an out-of-bounds read of plain data, because the failure mode is arbitrary code execution, not a crash.

Where in your own driver code does an index come from the wire before it's range-checked?

<b>2. Post: Regulatory shift — the EU deadline everyone was tracking quietly moved two months ago</b>
Today, Aug 30, was supposed to be the EU Cyber Resilience Act's first harmonized-standards deadline. It isn't, and hasn't been since early July.

<b>4. Post: Capability unlock — a cellular-grade wireless protocol just became free to actually try</b>
1 million devices per square kilometer. Sub-millisecond latency. And until this week, no way to run it without buying certified vendor silicon.

DECT NR+ (formally DECT-2020 NR) is a license-exempt standard built for exactly that density and latency class — mesh-capable, IPv6 or non-IP, years of battery life for sensors. Opener, released Aug 27 under a German-government-funded initiative, is the first fully open-source implementation of its MAC layer and above, written for Zephyr under Apache 2.0.

The unlock isn't the protocol's specs, which have existed for a while — it's that anyone can now read the actual MAC-layer source, port it, and experiment on real Zephyr hardware instead of taking a vendor's word for how the stack behaves.

<b>5. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — why a PLC microcontroller now ships with post-quantum crypto</b>
The threat isn't "a quantum computer breaks your MCU's encryption next year." It's "someone is recording your encrypted traffic right now, to decrypt it once one exists."

NXP's new MCX A5, announced this month, pairs a fairly ordinary 240MHz Cortex-M33 with two things that don't usually show up together on an industrial MCU: 10BASE-T1S single-pair Ethernet with automatic topology discovery, and a hardware post-quantum root of trust, at PSA Certified Level 3. It's targeting PLCs, remote I/O, and building/energy management gear — infrastructure with 15-20 year deployment lifetimes.

That last number is the actual design insight: post-quantum silicon isn't showing up first in phones or laptops, which get replaced every few years. It's showing up first in the industrial gear that will still be running, unpatched, in 2045 — a "harvest now, decrypt later" threat model applied to hardware refresh cycles instead of software ones.

If your product will still be in the field in 20 years, is its crypto silicon or firmware-upgradeable?
