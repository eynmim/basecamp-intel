<b>1. Post: Regulatory shift — the deadline is real, the portal isn't</b>
In three days, EU law requires connected-product makers to report exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours. As of the last confirmed check, the platform they're required to report through wasn't live yet.

ENISA confirmed on August 31 that its Single Reporting Platform — the sole channel recognised for EU Cyber Resilience Act Article 14 reporting — was still not operational, with multiple compliance trackers now expecting it to open the same day the obligation becomes mandatory: September 11. ENISA's own FAQ adds a second constraint: no API at launch, meaning every early warning, full notification, and final report has to go through a web form by hand.

The part worth sitting with as an engineer: this isn't a compliance-team problem you can ignore until it's someone else's job. If you ship anything with a radio and a CPU into the EU — including a side project or a thesis prototype someone downloads — you're the manufacturer the clock applies to.

Do you know which national CSIRT you'd file through if you had to, starting Monday?

<b>2. Post: Security wake-up — the "just a radio module" blind spot</b>
Thirteen chained authentication weaknesses, one CISA advisory, one LoRa module most engineers treat as a black box.

CISA published ICSA-26-239-05 on August 31 covering the Ebyte NA111-M, bundling issues like session tokens insufficiently protected during client-side handling, a vendor configuration utility that permits administrative access without verifying operator identity under certain conditions, and use of a deprecated hashing algorithm in the authentication path.

None of these are exotic. They're the kind of finding you'd expect from a device that never got the security review its main MCU firmware did — because "it's just the radio module" is a mental shortcut, not a security boundary. A LoRa module still runs its own firmware, exposes its own management interface, and sits on the same board as everything you did harden.

Worth an inventory check: which modules on your BOM have you actually audited versus just trusted?

<b>3. Post: Capability unlock — a chip whose only job is letting the MCU sleep</b>
200 nanoamps. That's the idle current of a chip Nanopower just launched whose entire purpose is making sure your real microcontroller almost never has to wake up.

The nPZ2100 sits between your sensors and your MCU, autonomously servicing up to six I²C/SPI peripherals and switching their power domains on its own — including local SRAM, an ADC, and event monitoring — so the host MCU stays fully powered down until something actually needs its attention. Development kits and samples ship this month.

This is a hardware answer to a problem most of us solve in software: FreeRTOS tickless idle and Zephyr's power management framework both try to minimize wake time, but they can't avoid waking the CPU core itself to decide whether a wake was even necessary. A companion IC that makes that decision before the MCU boots at all is a different point on the power-latency tradeoff entirely — and a good one to know by name.

Worth a datasheet read if battery life has ever been the reason a sensor-node feature got cut.

Wi-Fi 6E, Bluetooth LE 5.4, a Cortex-M33 core, and an AI/ML accelerator — Ezurio just put all of it in a module smaller than a fingernail, and it can run your whole application with no separate host chip.

<b>5. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — a secure boot check that only checks itself</b>
A "signed firmware" security feature that a new CVE just showed doesn't actually verify anything, because the thing it checks the signature against ships inside the file being checked.

CVE-2026-82876, disclosed August 31 with a CVSS of 8.2, affects the Phison PS3111-S11 SSD controller: firmware signature verification uses a public RSA modulus embedded in the firmware image itself, rather than one anchored in immutable, on-chip storage. An attacker can generate their own RSA key pair, sign a malicious firmware image with the private key, embed the matching modulus in that same image — and the controller accepts it as legitimately signed.

The underlying lesson generalizes past this one controller: a signature check is only as strong as where the trusted public key lives. If the key (or the modulus that stands in for it) is stored anywhere an attacker can modify alongside the payload, "signed" stops meaning "verified" and starts meaning "internally consistent" — which is a very different, much weaker guarantee.

Where does your project's root-of-trust key actually live — and can it be rewritten from the same interface that rewrites your firmware?
