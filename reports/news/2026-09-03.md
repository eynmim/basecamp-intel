<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Eight days before the EU Cyber Resilience Act's Article 14 vulnerability-and-incident reporting obligation takes effect, ENISA has confirmed that its Single Reporting Platform (SRP) — the mandatory portal every manufacturer with a product on the EU market must file through — goes live on September 11, 2026: the exact same day filing through it becomes mandatory. The reporting clock itself doesn't move: 24 hours for an early warning, 72 hours for a full technical notification, 14 days for a final report, applying to any actively exploited vulnerability or severe incident in a product already on the market, not just new designs. ENISA has published registration, notification-submission, and platform-interface guides through August and has offered dry-run support, but none of that lets a team actually log in, register, or submit a real report before the clock starts. Why it matters: for a small shop or a student project scaling into a shipped product, "test the process in advance" is not an option this time — the only rehearsal available is the real thing, on day one of enforcement.

<b>1. Post: Regulatory shift — a deadline and its own portal share a birthday</b>
The compliance deadline and the portal you're supposed to file through go live on the exact same day.

September 11, 2026 is when the EU Cyber Resilience Act's Article 14 vulnerability-and-incident reporting obligation takes effect — 24 hours for an early warning, 72 hours for a full notification, 14 days for a final report. ENISA has confirmed its Single Reporting Platform, the mandatory filing system, goes live that same date.

Registration guides, notification-submission guides, and platform-interface documentation have been public since early August, and ENISA has offered dry-run support. But none of that lets a manufacturer actually log in, register, and submit a real report before the clock starts running on real incidents.

If you maintain a firmware repo that could plausibly become a shipped product, this is the week to have your disclosure process written down — not tested against the platform, since nobody gets to test it first.

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — an image processor that no longer needs a camera</b>
You can now feed Espressif's image signal processor a raw image that never came from a camera.

ESP-IDF v6.0.3, released September 2, adds ISP support for RAW image input straight from memory — no sensor interface required. That turns image-pipeline debugging from "reproduce it on real hardware" into "load a captured frame and iterate," and opens the door to synthetic test images for anyone validating color-conversion or noise-reduction settings offline.

The same release quietly closes something more urgent: an HTTP request-smuggling gap caused by incorrect Content-Length handling in the built-in HTTP server, now capped by a new HTTPD_MAX_REQ_BODY_LEN_KB setting. If your ESP32-S3 project exposes that server to any network at all, that's the line to check first.

Two very different kinds of "unlock" in one point release — a debugging convenience and a security fix that shouldn't wait for your next sprint.

<b>3. Post: Security wake-up — a kernel buffer overrun that needs no privilege at all</b>
Any process that can open a UDP socket can now write past the end of a kernel buffer — no container, no special privilege.

CVE-2026-53362, added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on August 27, is an incorrect parameter-length calculation in the Linux kernel's IPv6 fragmentation path. A local process with ordinary UDP-socket permissions can trigger an out-of-bounds kernel-memory write, opening a path to privilege escalation — CVSS 7.8.

What makes this one worth a second look for embedded folks: it lives in mainline kernel code that ships unmodified in a lot of OpenWrt-based gateways and Yocto BSPs, and the usual "restrict unprivileged namespaces" mitigation doesn't touch it, because the vulnerable path never needed a namespace to begin with.

If your product ships an embedded-Linux gateway on a 6.1 or 6.6-series kernel, check it against your vendor's patch status today.

<b>4. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — 200 nanoamps of "don't wake the MCU"</b>
A chip that draws 200 nanoamps just moved the "when does the MCU wake up" decision off the MCU entirely.

Nanopower Semiconductor's nPZ2100, covered by EDN on September 1, autonomously polls up to six I2C/SPI peripherals and switches four 1mA peripheral rails plus a 10mA host rail — all while idling at 200nA and drawing roughly 1uA during active polling. The host processor stays fully powered down until there's something worth waking up for.

The architectural shift is subtler than the power numbers suggest: instead of your firmware's low-power state machine deciding when to poll a sensor, that logic now lives in silicon that never needs to boot an instruction pipeline to do it — the same pattern as a hardware watchdog, applied to sensor scheduling instead of fault recovery.

Dev kits ship this month; volume production is targeted for Q2 2027.

Where in your own power budget is the MCU waking up just to ask "is anything interesting happening yet?"

<b>5. Post: Integration leap — one edge-AI architecture, two different lifecycles</b>
Qualcomm just drew a line between the edge-AI chip you'd put in a smart speaker and the one you'd put in an oil refinery.

Announced September 1, the Dragonwing Q-2390 targets consumer and commercial AIoT — smart home devices, retail terminals — bundling app compute, local AI acceleration, camera and display I/O, LTE Cat 4, and GNSS into one part. Its sibling IQ-2390 opens a new "IQ2 Series" built specifically for industrial edge equipment, where the design priority shifts from peak performance to guaranteed long-term availability.

That's the integration leap worth noticing: it's not just AI compute and connectivity merging into one SoC anymore, it's a vendor explicitly forking the same architecture into a consumer roadmap and an industrial roadmap with different lifecycle guarantees, rather than selling one part into both markets.

Both chips are in early access only — public eval kits aren't expected until early 2027.
