CERT Polska and Shadowserver confirmed active, ongoing exploitation of a two-CVE chain in MikroTik RouterOS dubbed "MikroTrick": CVE-2026-67276 lets an attacker who knows a username and the public modulus of its authorized SSH key craft a different key that RouterOS wrongly accepts, because the auth check compares only the key type and modulus and skips the exponent, and CVE-2026-86060 then escalates a low-privilege session to full admin via a specially crafted username. <b>1. Post: Security wake-up — a 9.3 that breaks the one thing your MPU was supposed to guarantee</b>
If your FreeRTOS design uses the MPU to wall off an untrusted task from the rest of the system, a 9.3-severity bug just showed that wall could be walked through.

CVE-2026-77234 lives in the software-timer command path: an unprivileged task can reach a code path meant to be reachable only from inside the kernel, and use it to execute arbitrary code with full privileges — on MPU-enabled ports specifically, the exact configuration you'd choose because you wanted isolation.

The part that stands out: this isn't a buffer overflow or a missing bounds check, it's a command handler that never verified who was allowed to call it. Three related bugs (a TrustZone secure-context use-after-free, a secure-heap overflow, a queue-set info leak) shipped in the same fix, all in FreeRTOS-Kernel V11.3.1.

If you're relying on an RTOS's MPU support for task isolation, when did you last verify your kernel version against its actual CVE list, not just "it's stable, it's fine"?

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — same silicon, one GPU, two very different products</b>
Renesas just shipped two chips that are pin-for-pin identical except for one design choice: whether the GPU is there at all.

The RZ/G3L and RZ/G3SE are 64-bit MPUs with up to four Cortex-A55 cores at 1.2 GHz and a 200 MHz Cortex-M33 real-time core — pin-compatible with each other. The RZ/G3L keeps a Mali-G31 GPU and H.264 codec for HMI work like retail terminals; the RZ/G3SE strips both out for IoT gateways and EV chargers that don't need a display pipeline, and both idle down to roughly 1 mW in deep standby.

That's the real story: pin-compatibility turns "do we need a GPU" from a board-respin decision into a BOM-line decision, made after the hardware is largely locked. It's a quiet but very deliberate piece of product-line architecture.

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — your connected product now needs a data API, not just a spec sheet</b>
As of yesterday, "the user can technically extract their data with effort" is no longer good enough for a new connected product sold in the EU.

The EU Data Act's Article 3(1) "access by design" obligation took effect September 12: any new connected product must let users access the data it generates "easily, securely, free of charge... continuously and in real time," in a structured, machine-readable format — which in practice means a secure API, authentication, and consent management designed in from the start, not added after a customer asks.

It lands one day after the Cyber Resilience Act's exploited-vulnerability reporting duty went live on September 11 — so a product shipped into the EU market now needs both a live data-access API and a 24-hour vulnerability-reporting pipeline ready at launch, not as a v2 feature.

If you were speccing a new connected device today, would "data access API" already be on your architecture diagram, or still a compliance afterthought?

<b>5. Post: Capability unlock — Bluetooth LE is about to move data 4x faster without spending more power</b>
2 Mbps has been Bluetooth LE's ceiling for years. The Bluetooth SIG's draft spec for High Data Throughput pushes that to 7.5 Mbps — without giving up the low-power behavior that makes LE usable on a coin cell in the first place.

HDT gets there through new modulation, forward error correction, larger packets, and block-based transmission rather than just cranking up radio power. The SIG's own estimate: a multi-megabyte OTA firmware update that takes about 16 seconds today could drop to roughly 4 seconds once HDT lands, expected in the next Core Specification release later this year.

The part worth sitting with: throughput and power draw have usually traded off against each other in LE designs. If HDT actually holds the power budget while nearly quadrupling throughput, that's not a spec bump, it's a shift in what "low-power" and "high-bandwidth" get to mean in the same design.

What would you build differently if OTA updates and sensor-data dumps over BLE stopped being the slow part of your firmware?
