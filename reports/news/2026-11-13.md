<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Attacks on supermarket refrigeration controllers have become Europe's clearest example of embedded-device tampering with physical effects. A first wave on 11–12 Oct pushed units in about 700 French stores to as high as 50 °C, and France has attributed it to Shahid Kaveh, a unit affiliated with Iran's IRGC. A second wave on 2 Nov hit supermarkets and food businesses in at least nine countries, Italy among them: Coop Sweden reported 7 of 800 stores affected, Delhaize several dozen stores in Belgium and Luxembourg, and an ICA store had units set to 50 °C. Reporting on the vendor conflicts: Danfoss AK-SM / AK-SC controllers and a Belgian contractor are both named, and Danfoss says the affected units ran outdated software. Nobody has claimed the November wave. On 10 Nov ENISA's executive director warned that home appliances could be next. ENISA and the CSIRTs Network advise checking whether controllers are reachable from the internet and applying updates. Why it matters: the entry point was remote management of old firmware on internet-reachable controllers, not a sophisticated exploit. Secure-by-default remote access and long-term update support are now product requirements, not extras.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the fridge that heated to 50 °C</b>
Attackers did not need a zero-day to raise supermarket freezers to 50 °C. They needed an internet-reachable controller running old firmware.

Across two waves, in October in France and on 2 November in at least nine European countries, refrigeration controllers were remotely reconfigured. Stock spoiled. Coop Sweden alone reported 7 of 800 stores affected.

The engineering lesson: these are embedded devices with a 10–15 year service life, managed remotely by contractors. Reports say the affected units ran outdated software. The real failure is how the product was maintained over its lifetime, not one bug.

If you ship a device with remote access, the question isn't "is it secure today?" It's "who updates it in year 9?"

How does your team plan firmware support beyond the warranty?

<b>2. Post: Regulatory shift — 24 hours to report</b>
If your product has a digital element and sells in the EU, you now have 24 hours.

Since 11 September 2026, the Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers to report actively exploited vulnerabilities through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform: an early warning in 24 h, a full notification in 72 h, then a final report.

The engineering consequence is that you can't report what you can't detect. You need a working SBOM, a way to map a new CVE to the firmware versions in the field, and an OTA path to fix it. Those used to be nice-to-haves. Now they are what lets you meet the deadline at all.

Who owns the 24-hour clock in your organisation: legal, security, or the firmware team?

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — isolation lives in the kernel</b>
An MPU or TrustZone gives you a hardware wall. Eight new ThreadX CVEs show the wall is only as good as the code at the gate.

The 1 Nov hotfix to Eclipse ThreadX's Module Manager fixes unchecked pointers in TrustZone-M entry functions, a module that could control threads it does not own, and a Cortex-M port that saved registers through an unchecked stack pointer while privileged.

The insight: hardware enforces where code can go, but the kernel decides what a sandboxed module can ask it to do. Every pointer that crosses the boundary has to be validated by the privileged side. Miss one, and the sandbox is decoration.

Do you fuzz your RTOS syscall boundary, or trust the vendor port?

https://github.com/eclipse-threadx/threadx/releases

<b>4. Post: Tooling unlock — Espressif picks one IDE</b>
Espressif just told ESP32 developers where to build: Espressif-IDE 4.5.0 is the last feature release.

The Eclipse-based IDE now only gets security and high-severity fixes until October 2027, and is not recommended for new projects. The path forward is the ESP-IDF extension for VS Code.

Why this matters more than it sounds: one vendor-supported front end means docs, examples and debug flows converge on a single place. And because project-level settings live in the ESP-IDF project, not the IDE, the move is mostly painless. That only holds if your build was <code>idf.py</code>-clean in the first place.

<b>5. Post: Integration leap — motor control meets the NPU</b>
The motor-control MCU and the inference engine are becoming the same chip.

TI is showing NPU-equipped real-time MCUs at electronica, in the AM13Ex line that pairs a Cortex-M33 and its TinyEngine NPU with motor-control hardware such as high-resolution PWM. TI claims up to 90x lower latency and 120x lower energy per inference than running the same model without the accelerator.

The engineering angle: fault detection on motor current (bearing wear, imbalance) used to mean streaming data to a separate processor. On one die the model sits next to the control loop's own ADC samples, and the hard part becomes scheduling inference so it never adds jitter to the loop.

Would you trust inference on the same core that closes your current loop?
