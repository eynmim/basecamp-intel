<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
NSA, CISA, the FBI, DOE, and EPA jointly issued Advisory AA26-231A on August 19 warning of an active, ongoing campaign against internet-exposed Siemens S7 series PLCs (S7-200/300/400/1200/1500 plus F-series safety controllers). <b>1. Post: Security wake-up — feds confirm the exploit code hitting your PLC might be AI-written</b>
Federal agencies just put something in writing that security researchers have warned about for years: the exploit code targeting your industrial controller may not have a human author.

On August 19, NSA, CISA, the FBI, DOE, and EPA jointly warned of an active campaign against internet-exposed Siemens S7 PLCs. Attackers scan the internet with tools like Censys and ZoomEye, then deploy exploitation scripts the agencies describe as AI-generated and disguised as legitimate OT monitoring utilities.

The engineering detail that matters isn't "AI wrote malware" as a headline — it's that AI-assisted exploit generation collapses the time between "someone finds your device on Shodan/Censys" and "someone has working code against it." If your threat model still assumes obscurity buys you time before a hand-crafted exploit shows up, that assumption just got a lot weaker.

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — a SIM card can now run code on your modem before your OS even sees the network</b>
A SIM card was never supposed to be a code-execution vector. Researchers just showed it is one, on cellular IoT devices you've probably used.

University of Birmingham and Fuzzware researchers built a toolkit called CATana to explore SIM Toolkit's "Proactive SIM" feature — a legitimate mechanism letting a SIM send AT commands directly into a device's modem baseband. Tracked as CVE-2026-57550, they confirmed it against Quectel's EC25, EG25, and RM52xN series, chips that show up in EV chargers, industrial routers, and connected cars.

The unsettling part isn't the exploit, it's the trust model it exposes: the modem was built to treat anything arriving over the SIM interface as trusted network signaling, not untrusted input. That's the same class of assumption embedded engineers make constantly about "internal" interfaces — until someone controls the other end.

If your design has a cellular module, do you know what your SIM interface is implicitly allowed to do to it?

<b>3. Post: Tooling unlock — Espressif just gave Rust the same SDK status as C on its RISC-V chips</b>
Ten years ago, Rust on a microcontroller meant fighting the toolchain. In 2026, esp-rs reportedly carries the same first-class support tier as Espressif's own C/C++ SDK on the RISC-V ESP32 line.

A mid-2026 review of embedded Rust's state notes esp-rs now drives ESP32-C6, H2, and P4 development, probe-rs has effectively replaced OpenOCD as the default debug-probe driver, and Embassy 0.6 has become the default async runtime — a genuine no_std alternative to reaching for FreeRTOS by default.

The insight worth sitting with: async/await as a scheduling model sidesteps a whole category of RTOS task-priority and stack-sizing decisions you'd normally make up front. It doesn't replace FreeRTOS everywhere, but it changes what "the easy default" looks like for a new ESP32 or RP2350 project.

Would you trust an async runtime over a preemptive RTOS for your next sensor node?

<b>5. Post: Architecture insight — 14,500 cameras got hijacked through a feature, not a bug</b>
No firmware exploit. No zero-day. Attackers took over more than 14,500 Dahua devices largely by using the vendor's own remote-access feature exactly as designed.

Hunt.io traced a 35-day campaign, Operation CameraSwarm, that combined credential stuffing and two 2021 authentication-bypass CVEs with something more interesting: abuse of Dahua's P2P remote-access relay, which lets a client reach a device behind NAT using only its serial number, before any device-level authentication happens.

The lesson generalizes past Dahua: any P2P or cloud-relay feature you build into a product to solve "customers hate port-forwarding" is, by construction, a pre-authentication tunnel into your device. If the relay's only gate is a serial number or device ID, you've built a documented bypass of your own network perimeter.

Does your product's remote-access relay authenticate before or after it opens the tunnel?
