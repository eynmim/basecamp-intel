<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Schneider Electric's September 2026 ICS Patch Tuesday disclosed CVE-2026-3869, a CVSS v4.0 9.2 authentication-bypass flaw (CWE-303, incorrect implementation of an authentication algorithm) in the Modicon M580 and M580 Safety programmable logic controllers. Every M580 running an application level below 4.00 is affected (below 4.20 for the Safety variant), and remediation isn't a simple patch install — it requires upgrading the controller's application level itself to 4.00/4.20 or higher via Schneider's support portal. The disclosure landed in the same week the EU Cyber Resilience Act's 24-hour exploited-vulnerability reporting duty went live (September 11), making this one of the first major ICS vulnerabilities to surface inside that new mandatory-reporting regime. A PLC like the M580 is embedded firmware controlling real industrial hardware, not a desktop app — an auth-bypass here means an attacker on the OT network can potentially rewrite the ladder logic driving physical equipment. Why it matters: this is the same underlying bug class as this month's RouterOS and FreeRTOS disclosures — an authentication check that was implemented wrong rather than missing outright — making "logic error in the auth path" this quarter's dominant embedded vulnerability pattern.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — a PLC auth check that was wrong, not missing</b>
A CVSS 9.2 just landed on an industrial PLC, and the bug isn't a buffer overflow — it's an authentication algorithm that was implemented incorrectly.

CVE-2026-3869 hits Schneider Electric's Modicon M580 and M580 Safety controllers: any unit running an application level below 4.00 (4.20 for Safety) has an authentication check that doesn't do what it's supposed to. Fixing it means upgrading the controller's application level, not just installing a patch.

What stands out to me: this is the third time this month I've seen this exact bug shape — RouterOS, FreeRTOS's MPU privilege escalation, now a PLC. The auth code exists, it just doesn't check the right thing. That's a harder class of bug to catch in review than a missing bounds check, because the logic looks complete at a glance.

If you're reviewing auth code on embedded hardware, when did you last trace every field the check actually compares, not just confirm a check exists?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — vision transformers just moved down to MCU-class silicon</b>
Running a vision transformer used to mean a full Linux SoC. Amlogic's new chips argue otherwise.

The A123X and C305X2 are the first shipping parts built on Arm's Cortex-A320 — a 34% scalar performance jump and a 10x ML uplift over Cortex-A35 via SVE2. Paired with an 8 TOPS NPU that natively handles Transformer operations, not just CNNs, both chips are aimed at cameras, robots and battery-powered AIoT devices.

The part I keep coming back to: "compact LLM on a camera module" was a stretch goal a year ago. Putting native Transformer support in the NPU, rather than bolting it onto a CNN accelerator, is what actually makes that practical at this power and cost tier — it's an architecture decision, not just a bigger number on a spec sheet.

<b>5. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — an IC that manages sensors so your MCU can stay off</b>
200 nanoamps. That's the idle draw of a chip whose entire job is deciding when your "real" microcontroller is allowed to wake up.

Nanopower's nPZ2100 is a companion IC, not a microcontroller — it autonomously polls sensors, tracks timing, and checks thresholds, only interrupting the host MCU when something actually needs a decision. The host processor, the power-hungry part of the system, stays powered down until there's real work.

The architectural insight here isn't the low current figure by itself — MCUs already have deep-sleep modes with similar draw. It's the division of labor: a purpose-built supervisor that's electrically incapable of doing anything except watch sensors is a fundamentally different power budget than a general-purpose core running a "low power" firmware loop.

Where in your own designs is the MCU doing supervisor work that a dedicated companion chip could do for a fraction of the current?
