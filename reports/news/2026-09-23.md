<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the switch bug that put Italy on an exploited-device list</b>
996 switches, 48 countries, one unauthenticated buffer overflow. Italy was on that list.

CISA confirmed this week that CVE-2026-7273, a stack-based overflow in the CGI web-management handler on Zyxel's GS1900 switches, has been actively exploited since mid-August by a suspected Chinese-speaking actor. No login needed — just a crafted HTTP request to a switch reachable on the LAN, then a TFTP pull for a script that harvests config files and hashed root credentials.

What gets me isn't the sophistication — there isn't any. It's an old-school unauthenticated CGI handler with no bounds check, the exact pattern a lot of embedded web-config pages still ship with. "It's only reachable on the LAN" stops being a real boundary the moment any of these devices sit behind a router with remote-management port forwarding turned on.

If your firmware exposes a web UI, when did you last actually try to overflow it yourself?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — neuromorphic edge AI now clips onto an Arduino for $99</b>
A spiking neural network accelerator used to mean a research grant. Now it's a $99 SPI module.

BrainChip's AKD1500 neuromorphic co-processor — event-driven, sub-300 mW, up to 800 GOPS — just shipped in a new BrainBoard 1500 form factor built specifically for Arduino Nicla boards, alongside M.2 and PCIe cards for Raspberry Pi 5 and standard PCs.

The interesting part is the architecture, not the price. An event-driven spiking network only computes when the input changes, so an always-on sensor — audio, vibration, gesture — can run inference continuously at milliwatt power instead of waking a full MCU core to poll and classify. That's a genuinely different compute model from bolting an NPU onto a Cortex-M, and now it's cheap enough to actually prototype with.

Worth a weekend with a Nicla board to see where spiking inference beats a TFLite Micro model on your own sensor data?

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — splitting one Bluetooth SoC into two brains</b>
Realtek's new Bluetooth chip doesn't have one core doing everything. It has two, deliberately kept apart.

The RTL8773J pairs a 200 MHz Cortex-M55 application core with a separate Cortex-M33 handling sensors and the network stack, plus dedicated DSPs for codec processing and an accelerator block just for ANC, Talk Clear and keyword spotting. It also carries a draft-stage feature called Bluetooth HDT (High Data Throughput), which the Bluetooth SIG hasn't even ratified into a Core Specification yet — expected late this year — pushing BLE from 2 Mbps up toward 7.5 Mbps.

The design choice worth noticing: keeping the radio/network core separate from the application core means the always-on audio and connectivity workload can't get starved by whatever the app core is doing, and vice versa. It's the same tradeoff behind any dual-core wireless SoC, just applied here at a tighter integration level.

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — the FCC just tightened who can certify your hardware</b>
A public-comment window most embedded engineers never look at just closed, and it could affect which components you're legally allowed to ship.

The FCC's proposed rule on protecting the communications supply chain through its Equipment Authorization Program closed its reply-comment period on September 21. It targets national-security risk inside the certification pipeline every Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and cellular module needs to clear before it can legally ship in a US product — the same FCC ID process behind every module datasheet's compliance section.

The engineering-relevant angle: FCC authorization already constrains your BOM, not just your paperwork, because a module vendor's own FCC ID (or lack of one) decides whether you can sell a finished product built on it. A tightened authorization pipeline can mean fewer eligible module vendors, longer certification lead times for new parts, or added scrutiny on modules from specific countries — any of which shows up as a supply-chain constraint on your next design, not just a compliance checkbox.

Worth checking now whether your go-to Wi-Fi/BLE module's FCC grant depends on a supplier this rule might touch.
