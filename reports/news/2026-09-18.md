<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the "encrypted" camera that stored its own key next to the footage</b>
A hardware teardown just proved that "encrypted storage" is a claim, not a fact — you have to check where the key lives.

Researchers physically pulled Flock Safety license-plate cameras off poles and dumped their storage. They found two unencrypted partitions on the device — one held vendor files, the other held the encryption key protecting the "encrypted" media partition. The cameras were also still running Android 8.1, an OS build that's been out of security support since 2021, with hardcoded credentials on top.

None of the individual failures here are exotic. Hardcoded credentials, an EOL OS, a key stored next to the data it protects — each one is a known anti-pattern. What made this exploitable wasn't one clever bug, it was that all three shipped together on a device deployed at scale, for years, with nobody physically auditing it.

If you're designing anything with a secure element or a key-storage requirement: where does the key actually live, and who's checked?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — a real NPU on an MCU, for the price of a nice lunch</b>
Running an actual neural accelerator on a microcontroller used to mean picking an expensive eval board. That changed this week.

Alif Semiconductor's new Balletto B1 and Ensemble E1C StartKits pair a 160 MHz Cortex-M55 with an Ethos-U55 NPU, 2MB of SRAM and up to 1.9MB of non-volatile memory — on low-cost development boards, not a $500 eval kit.

The distinction that matters: this isn't software-optimized inference on a fast CPU (what most ESP32-class boards give you today). Ethos-U55 is dedicated NPU silicon, and Arm's CMSIS-NN + Ethos-U toolchain targets it directly. That changes what's actually feasible for battery-powered always-on inference — wake-word detection, vibration classification, gesture sensing — without a CPU running flat-out.

Worth a weekend if you've only ever done TinyML on general-purpose MCU silicon.

<b>3. Post: Tooling unlock — one embedded IDE, no more OS-switching for CI</b>
Most embedded teams still develop on Windows and build on Linux CI — and quietly maintain two toolchains to make that work.

IAR just extended its certified Embedded Workbench platform with native Linux support alongside Windows, built on Qt with direct VS Code integration and CMake-based workflows. Same certified compiler, same debugger, same IDE — just running natively wherever your pipeline actually lives.

The unglamorous part is the real value: safety-critical toolchains are certified as specific, frozen configurations. Every time a team bridges Windows-dev/Linux-CI with extra scripting or a second toolchain install, that's a gap between "what's certified" and "what's actually running." Removing the OS boundary removes that gap, not just a convenience.

Small news, real DX difference if you've ever debugged a build that only failed in CI.

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — sanctions law is a spec sheet you can't ignore</b>
Export control law reads nothing like a datasheet, but if you're building or shipping hardware, it's just as binding.

The UK's Iran (Sanctions) (Amendment) Regulations 2026 take effect September 29, expanding trade prohibitions on goods, technology and software tied to specific sectors, alongside new financial restrictions — following the EU's own re-imposed nuclear-related sanctions and dual-use export controls earlier this year.

The engineering-relevant detail most people skip: "dual-use" export control language routinely sweeps in things that don't look like weapons at all — certain RF/wireless modules, crypto-capable firmware, and specific semiconductor categories. If your BOM includes parts in those categories and your supply chain or customer base touches a sanctioned jurisdiction, that's a compliance question, not a legal-team-only question.

Worth actually reading the schedule of controlled goods once, rather than assuming it doesn't apply to your project.

<b>5. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — getting a quad-core Linux SoC under 1mW standby</b>
A four-core application processor that idles at roughly a milliwatt sounds like a contradiction. Renesas just shipped it anyway.

The new RZ/G3L and RZ/G3SE MPUs pack up to four Cortex-A55 cores at 1.2 GHz plus a 200 MHz Cortex-M33 real-time coprocessor, with standby power dropping to around 1 mW and fast resume from deep standby — aimed squarely at industrial HMI and IoT edge systems that need embedded Linux but can't tolerate always-on power draw.

The architectural trick isn't just process-node scaling — it's the split itself. The M33 coprocessor stays alive to handle sensing and wake conditions while the A55 cluster is fully powered down, so "runs Linux" and "sub-mW standby" stop being mutually exclusive requirements. That's the same big.LITTLE-adjacent logic showing up further down the power curve every generation.

Where's the line, in your own designs, between "needs a real OS" and "needs to sleep at nanowatts"?
