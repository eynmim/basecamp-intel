<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
The European Commission published its first official application guidance for the Cyber Resilience Act on July 27 — a package running past 80 pages with dozens of worked examples covering product scope, how open-source components are treated, what counts as a "substantial modification," and how to determine a product's support period. The clarification that matters most for firmware teams: the test for a substantial modification is whether a change alters the product's cybersecurity risk profile (new attack surface, new threat vectors), not how big the change is — so a routine OTA security patch is not, by itself, substantial, but adding a new radio or opening a new network service likely is. It also draws a clearer line between an open-source maintainer (lighter regime) and a commercial manufacturer shipping that code in a product (full CRA obligations), the exact ambiguity that had stalled a lot of hobbyist-to-commercial roadmaps. The guidance lands six weeks before the first hard deadline: from September 11, 2026, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and serious incidents to ENISA within 24 hours of becoming aware of them. For anyone building IoT/embedded products for the EU market — including student projects that might eventually commercialize — this is the first concrete rulebook for what "secure by design" requires in practice, not just a date on a calendar.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — one filesystem library, eight frameworks exposed</b>
One tiny filesystem library. Zero patches. Eight different embedded frameworks quietly exposed.

runZero disclosed seven vulnerabilities in FatFs — the compact FAT/exFAT library baked into Espressif ESP-IDF, STM32Cube, Zephyr, MicroPython, Mbed, RIOT, ArduPilot and RT-Thread. The worst, CVE-2026-6682 (CVSS 7.6), is an integer overflow in FAT32 mounting that's reachable through a crafted USB drive, SD card, or in some cases a firmware-update path.

The real story isn't the bug, it's the supply chain around it: FatFs has no CVE history, no security mailing list, and no patch-notification mechanism, so every downstream project has to independently rediscover and fix the same issue.

If your project mounts FAT/exFAT media from user-supplied storage, have you actually checked which FatFs revision you're shipping?

<b>2. Post: Regulatory shift — the EU just gave firmware teams a real test to apply</b>
The EU just published the rulebook for what "secure by design" actually means — and it hinges on one test.

The European Commission's first official Cyber Resilience Act guidance (published July 27) clarifies open-source treatment and, more usefully, defines what counts as a "substantial modification" that resets a product's compliance obligations.

The test isn't the size of the change — it's whether the change alters the product's cybersecurity risk profile. A routine OTA security patch generally doesn't trigger it; adding a new radio or opening a new network service probably does. That's a genuinely useful mental model for how you structure firmware update strategies, not just a compliance checkbox.

Six weeks out from the first hard deadline (24-hour vulnerability reporting from September 11), is your team actually tracking which of your recent updates would count as substantial?
