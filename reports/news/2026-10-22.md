<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — The Wi-Fi blob you can't rebuild</b>
A Wi-Fi chip flaw reportedly gives root over the air, and for some chips there is no fix at all.

The report describes Broadcom's WPS code, shared across routers from dozens of vendors. Newer Linux-based models can be patched if the maker ships a Wi-Fi software update. Older eCos-based chips reportedly have no usable fix.

The engineering lesson is about patchability, not the bug. If the radio firmware is a blob you cannot rebuild, your update story depends on someone else's roadmap.

Do you track which closed components in your product you cannot patch yourself?

https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/unpatched-broadcom-wi-fi-flaw-lets.html

#cybersecurity #embedded #IoT #firmware

<b>2. Post: Tooling unlock — A bootloader default that changes your board</b>
Zephyr 4.5 reportedly makes signed, revertible MCUboot images the default on Espressif boards.

Draft notes flag it as breaking for board maintainers. Previously, a plain flash-and-run image was the easy path on these boards.

The insight: a secure-by-default bootloader quietly moves OTA rollback and signing from "later" to "day one". That is good for products and annoying for quick experiments.

Would you take safe defaults over a faster first blink?

https://docs.zephyrproject.org/latest/releases/release-notes-4.5.html

#Zephyr #ESP32 #embedded #firmware

<b>3. Post: Regulatory shift — SBOMs arrive in the RTOS</b>
Zephyr 4.5's draft notes list SPDX 3.0 SBOM generation.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act pushes manufacturers toward knowing exactly what is inside their firmware. An RTOS that emits the bill of materials at build time turns a paperwork task into a build artifact.

The catch is that an SBOM only helps if you actually read it when the next CVE lands.

Who in your team owns the SBOM after the first release?

<b>4. Post: Capability unlock — Analog front end on the MCU</b>
HPMicro's HPM5100 packs four 16-bit ADCs and four op-amps next to a 400 MHz RISC-V core.

For motor control and sensing, that moves part of the analog chain from the PCB into silicon. Fewer discrete parts, shorter analog traces, simpler layout.

The trade-off I would weigh is ecosystem: tooling, drivers and community matter as much as the datasheet when you are the one debugging at midnight.

<b>5. Post: Integration leap — Two RISC-V cores, two jobs</b>
ESP32-S31 pairs a 320 MHz RISC-V core with a low-power RISC-V core, and it is now showing up on third-party boards.

The pattern is familiar from ESP32-S3 work: let the small core watch sensors while the big one sleeps. What changes is that both cores share one ISA.

That could simplify toolchains and code sharing between the always-on and burst-processing sides.
