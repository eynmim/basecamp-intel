Seven unpatched vulnerabilities are reported in the FatFs filesystem library, which ships in devices from cameras and drones to industrial controllers and hardware crypto wallets. The attack vector is physical or file-based: a crafted SD card, USB stick or firmware update file can lead to memory corruption, data leakage, denial of service or code execution. <b>1. Post: Security wake-up — your SD card is an attack surface</b>
Seven unpatched bugs in one filesystem library, and a lot of devices ship it.

FatFs is the FAT/exFAT code behind cameras, drones, industrial controllers and even hardware crypto wallets. Researchers report seven vulnerabilities that give memory corruption, data leaks or code execution.

The part I find uncomfortable: the attacker does not need the network. A crafted SD card, USB stick or firmware update file is enough, because the parser trusts the on-disk structures.

We treat a filesystem image like data. It is really untrusted input to a parser written in C.

Do you validate media before mounting it, or do you trust the volume?

<b>2. Post: Integration leap — one chip, four radios</b>
The ESP32-S31 puts Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth 5.4 and 802.15.4 next to a Gigabit Ethernet MAC.

It is dual-core RISC-V: one high-performance core with FPU and SIMD, plus a separate low-power core. 62 GPIOs on top.

The engineering insight is the 802.15.4 part. Thread, Zigbee and Matter-over-Thread traffic no longer needs a second radio chip, so a gateway can shrink to one SoC and one firmware image.

I have spent time on the ESP32-S3, and the S31 reads like its gateway-class sibling.

Would you collapse your border router and bridge into one chip?

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — an NPU beside a Cortex-M0+</b>
TI says its TinyEngine NPU cuts inference latency up to 90x and energy over 120x. On a Cortex-M0+.

Those are vendor numbers against MCUs without an accelerator, so I would benchmark my own model before believing them.

But the direction matters. The M0+ is about as small as a 32-bit core gets, and the work moves to a dedicated block that does the multiply-accumulates in parallel at much lower energy per operation.

For TinyML that shifts the question from "can the CPU run this model" to "does my toolchain target the accelerator".

Which matters more to you, raw MACs or the toolchain around them?

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — CRA standards slip to year-end</b>
The EU Cyber Resilience Act still has no harmonised standard to lean on.

A draft amendment to the standardisation request would move the product-specific standards deadline from 30 October to 31 December 2026. It is a draft, so watch the final text.

What this means for firmware teams: a standard only gives presumption of conformity after it is cited in the Official Journal. Until then you map your product to the essential requirements yourself.

So secure boot, signed updates and a vulnerability-handling process are design inputs now, not paperwork later.

Is your team documenting its security design today, or waiting for the standard?

<b>5. Post: Capability unlock — Matter without Bluetooth</b>
Matter 1.4.2 lets a Wi-Fi device join the network without BLE.

Commissioning used to lean on Bluetooth LE for the first handshake. The new path uses Wi-Fi Unsynchronized Service Discovery instead.

The engineering consequence: a Wi-Fi-only Matter device no longer needs the BLE stack just for setup, which can mean less flash, less RAM and one fewer radio to certify.

BLE does not go away for other products, but its role in smart-home onboarding is shrinking.

Would you drop BLE from your next Wi-Fi Matter design?
