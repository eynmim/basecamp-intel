<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
CERT/CC note VU#188754 (published 9 Nov) shows how one firmware binary exposed a whole robot fleet. The FrodoBots EarthRover Mini+ firmware (6.2.4_6.2.5_0.614.0) ships fleet-wide secrets inside the binary customers receive: AWS access keys, AWS IoT MQTT credentials, an Agora token-service login and a Slack webhook (CVE-2026-121685). A firmware method executes shell commands received over MQTT as root, and the only thing gating it is that shared credential, so anyone who extracts the binary can command every rover (CVE-2026-121686, OS command injection). The same note lists an STM32 update path over plain HTTP with only an MD5 check, plus unauthenticated local interfaces. The vendor says it has revoked the cloud credentials and removed most embedded secrets and the arbitrary-shell path. No exploitation has been reported. Why it matters: this is the default architecture of many ESP32-plus-AWS-IoT products, where one provisioning shortcut turns a single device teardown into a fleet compromise.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — one key, a whole fleet</b>
Tear down one robot, and you could command all of them.

CERT/CC's note VU#188754 covers the FrodoBots EarthRover Mini+. The firmware customers receive contains fleet-wide AWS IoT MQTT credentials, AWS access keys and a Slack webhook. A firmware method runs shell commands that arrive over MQTT as root, and the shared credential is the only gate.

The engineering point: in a fleet, a credential that is identical on every device is not authentication. It only identifies the product, and extracting it from one unit means the whole fleet is exposed. Per-device certificates cost a provisioning step at the factory. Skipping that step costs you the fleet.

The vendor says it has revoked the keys and removed most embedded secrets.

How does your team provision device identity: per unit, or per product?

<b>2. Post: Tooling unlock — firmware tests without the board</b>
The hardest part of embedded CI is that the test rig is a physical board.

LabWired is an open-source simulator, written in Rust under MIT, that runs your compiled firmware on simulated MCU boards, both the CPU and the peripherals. It targets Arm Cortex-M and RISC-V and is designed to be deterministic, so the same binary gives the same trace on every run.

That determinism is the point. Hardware-in-the-loop tests are flaky because of timing, cables and board state. A deterministic simulator turns a flaky test into a reproducible test: one that is either green or red, and stays that way until the code changes.

It does not replace bring-up on real silicon. It does mean that every pull request can run the firmware itself, not just host-side unit tests.

What is your team's split between simulated and on-target tests?

<b>3. Post: Regulatory shift — the CRA gets its vocabulary on Tuesday</b>
On 17 Nov, the EU's Cyber Resilience Act is scheduled to get its first horizontal standards.

EN 40000-1-1 and EN 40000-1-2 are due for publication that day. They give the CRA's legal text something engineers can build to. Separately, 23 European industry groups are asking the Commission to cite EN IEC 62443 quickly, because industrial vendors have built their processes on 62443-4-1 and -4-2 for years.

One catch for engineers: a published standard is not yet a harmonised one. Until it is cited in the Official Journal, following it does not give presumption of conformity. So for now, test reports and design documents still have to argue compliance directly.

If you build connected products for the EU, which standard is your team writing its secure-development process against today?

<b>4. Post: Price-floor shift — industrial I/O for $21</b>
CAN, RS-485, Ethernet, Wi-Fi 6 and a camera interface now fit on a $21 board.

The Wireless-Tag WT99P461-S1 pairs an ESP32-P4X module with an ESP32-C61 as the radio. It adds Fast Ethernet, RS-485, CAN bus, MIPI CSI/DSI, a microphone and a speaker connector.

The architecture is the interesting part. The P4 has no radio, so the application processor and the wireless chip are separate parts, like a phone's application processor and modem. This keeps the real-time and HMI work on one chip and the Wi-Fi and BLE stack on the other, but you now have two firmware images to update and keep compatible.

For prototyping gateways, HMIs and fieldbus bridges, the cost of a capable dev platform has stopped being a factor.

<b>5. Post: Integration leap — LoRaWAN becomes utility infrastructure</b>
More than 30 million meters now report over LoRaWAN.

The LoRa Alliance says 150 million LoRaWAN devices are connected worldwide, with meters making up over 30 million of them, more than 700 certified devices and over 340 member organisations.

The firmware angle: utility tenders often require LoRaWAN alongside Wireless M-Bus, not instead of it. So a meter carries two radio stacks with very different timing, on a battery expected to last for years. Power budgeting, radio scheduling and update strategy, including how to update firmware over a link that carries a few bytes a day, become the core design problems.

Metering is turning into one of Europe's largest deployments of battery-powered embedded firmware.
