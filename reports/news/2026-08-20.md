<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
An independent researcher (handle 0x50594d) published a two-stage exploit chain against Unisoc T612 modem firmware that gives an attacker full Android kernel access after the victim answers a single VoLTE video call. The second-stage flaw, disclosed Aug 17 via SSD Secure Disclosure, chains onto a March 2026 RCE in the same modem firmware; the root cause is a lack of memory isolation between the cellular modem and the application processor's kernel, letting the attacker disable modem memory protections and pivot straight into kernel memory. There is no CVE, no patch, and no response from Unisoc after multiple outreach attempts by the researcher. Why it matters: this is a textbook case of what happens when a radio subsystem and the main SoC don't have a hard memory boundary between them — the same architectural question anyone bridging a cellular/LTE-Cat-M module to an MCU or embedded-Linux host needs to be able to answer about their own design.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — a video call is now a kernel exploit if your modem trusts your kernel too much</b>
Answer a video call, lose your Android kernel. No exploit code required on your end — just picking up.

An independent researcher chained two Unisoc T612 modem firmware bugs together: a March 2026 RCE in the SIP video-call parser, plus a new flaw disclosed this week that lets the attacker disable the modem's memory protections and reach straight into kernel memory. The root cause isn't a buffer overflow detail, it's architectural — there was never a hard memory boundary between the cellular modem and the application processor it talks to.

No CVE. No patch. Unisoc hasn't responded to the researcher's disclosure attempts. The lesson generalizes past this one chipset: any time you're bridging a radio subsystem (cellular, and this applies just as much to a smaller NB-IoT or Cat-M module bolted onto an MCU) into your main compute, "the radio stack is a black box that only sends me packets" is an assumption, not a guarantee.

If your design puts a cellular or LTE-Cat-M modem next to your MCU, do you actually know where the memory boundary is — or are you trusting the datasheet?

<b>2. Post: Integration leap — three radio chips just became one RISC-V SoC</b>
Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth 5.4, Thread, Zigbee, and Gigabit Ethernet used to mean three or four separate parts on your BOM. Espressif just shipped a chip that's all of them.

The ESP32-S31 — dual-core, 320MHz RISC-V, now in mass production with new orders landing through late August — folds Wi-Fi 6, BLE 5.4 with LE Audio, 802.15.4 (Thread + Zigbee), and a GbE MAC into a single SoC, with Matter reachable over either Wi-Fi or Thread. That's the smart-home-hub radio stack that used to need a dedicated Thread/Zigbee coprocessor sitting next to the main Wi-Fi/BLE part.

The engineering interesting bit isn't any single radio, it's that the coexistence problem — Wi-Fi, BLE, and 802.15.4 sharing one 2.4GHz die without stepping on each other — is now Espressif's problem to solve in silicon, not yours to solve in board layout and RF shielding.

Anyone else's BOM about to lose a coprocessor?

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — a chip that harvests, thinks, and senses off nothing but ambient light</b>
Most "battery-free IoT" designs still separate the problem into three chips: one to harvest energy, one to compute, one to sense. A Penn State team just stacked all three into one die.

Published in Nature Electronics, the chip is a three-layer IC that runs entirely on ambient light — no battery, no external power rail — while sensing chemicals in its surroundings and processing the result on-die. It's aimed squarely at the deployment case that kills most energy-harvesting IoT projects in practice: remote or hard-to-reach locations where "just replace the battery" isn't an option.

The architectural bet worth noticing: co-locating harvest, compute, and sense in one stack instead of three ICs on a board isn't just smaller, it removes the power-budget arguments between three separate power domains that usually eat most of an energy-harvesting design's headroom.

Would a single-stack harvest+compute+sense part change how you'd scope a battery-free sensor node?

<b>4. Post: Capability unlock — kilometre-range Wi-Fi just became a part you can actually order</b>
"Wi-Fi with LoRaWAN-like range" has been a slide-deck promise for years. This month it became a certified, orderable module.

Quectel's FGH200M, built on Morse Micro's MM8108 chipset, cleared FCC/IC/CE/RCM certification for mass production in late July. Morse Micro also shipped its own high-power MM8108-M20 module. <b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — Brussels just published the answer key for the Cyber Resilience Act</b>
The Cyber Resilience Act has a real deadline in three weeks. The Commission just published 67 examples explaining what compliance actually looks like.

On July 27, the European Commission issued its first official CRA application guidance — Communication C(2026) 5252 — with practical examples, flowcharts, and use cases aimed specifically at reducing ambiguity for smaller manufacturers and developers. It's not legally binding, but it's the Commission's own interpretation of a law whose vulnerability-reporting obligation goes live September 11.

The part worth internalizing as an engineer, not a compliance officer: the guidance keeps circling back to the same technical requirements — secure-by-default configuration, SBOM, and a working update-and-disclosure pipeline. Those aren't legal deliverables you bolt on later, they're firmware architecture decisions that are far cheaper to make before first silicon than after.

If you're shipping into the EU, does your current firmware pipeline already produce an SBOM, or would September 11 be the day you find out it doesn't?
