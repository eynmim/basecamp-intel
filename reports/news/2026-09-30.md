<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — 29 commands nobody documented</b>
The ESP32 Bluetooth controller shipped with 29 undocumented HCI commands.

Researchers found vendor commands in the chip's Bluetooth controller, including 0xFC02, which writes memory directly. It was tracked as CVE-2025-27840, CVSS 6.8.

The nuance matters: Espressif's own write-up stresses that exploitation needs access to the HCI transport such as UART or USB, or an already-compromised host. So it is less a remote backdoor and more a broken assumption that the controller is a sealed black box.

I now treat the HCI boundary as an attack surface in every design review.

Do you ever audit what your controller exposes below the host stack?

<b>2. Post: Regulatory shift — the 24-hour clock</b>
Since 11 September, EU manufacturers have 24 hours to report an actively exploited vulnerability.

That is the CRA reporting phase, and it already applies. The full obligations follow on 11 December 2027.

For firmware teams, the hard part isn't the form. It is knowing which dependency versions ship on which device. Without an SBOM and a tested OTA path, you can't answer a regulator, or a customer, in a day.

I'm treating SBOM generation and signed OTA as core firmware skills now, not extras.

Is your team ready to answer "which devices are affected?" within hours?

<b>3. Post: Capability unlock — an NPU inside an STM32</b>
About 600 GOPS at roughly 3 TOPS/W, on an STM32.

The STM32N6 is ST's first family with its in-house Neural-ART NPU, around 300 MAC units at 1 GHz.

What changes is the toolchain story: models compile through ST's own flow to a dedicated accelerator, rather than running as Cortex-M loops. That moves vision workloads that used to need an application processor onto an MCU-class part.

The trade-off is another vendor-specific compiler to learn, alongside Arm Ethos-U and TI's TinyEngine.

<b>5. Post: Physics meets architecture — harvesting from 5 µW</b>
A harvesting PMIC that cold-starts from 5 µW at 275 mV.

e-peas' AEM15820 harvests from hybrid indoor and outdoor PV cells, from microwatts to watts, on one chip. It regulates 0.6–3.3 V at up to 100 mA and works with batteries and lithium-ion capacitors.

The insight is that indoor light and sunlight used to need different front ends. One IC covering both means a BLE sensor node can stay battery-free indoors and still handle a bright windowsill.

The firmware consequence is a duty-cycle budget driven by harvest, not by a fixed sleep timer.

Have you designed a node around harvested energy rather than a battery?
