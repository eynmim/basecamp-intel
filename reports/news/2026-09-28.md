<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
CISA published ICSA-26-267-02 on September 24, disclosing three chained vulnerabilities in Eufy's Omni C20 and Omni X10 Pro robot vacuums (firmware before 1.6.4): an unauthenticated command injection reachable during the BLE/Wi-Fi pairing process (CVE-2026-93289, CVSS 7.5), hardcoded credentials logged in plaintext that expose floor-plan/mapping data (CVE-2026-93290), and — the critical one — Omni C20 skipping TLS certificate validation entirely (CVE-2026-93291, CVSS 9.4), letting an on-path attacker MITM the connection and push arbitrary code. Eufy has shipped 1.6.4 with fixes; CISA reports no known in-the-wild exploitation yet. All three bugs sit in the exact flow an embedded engineer designs by hand: device commissioning. Trusting the pairing handshake, skipping cert pinning "for now," and logging credentials for debugging are textbook shortcuts that map directly onto BLE/Wi-Fi provisioning code most IoT teams write themselves rather than pull from a vetted SDK. Why it matters: this isn't an exotic firmware bug class — it's three separate corners cut in the one part of the stack (provisioning) that every connected-product team eventually has to build in-house.

<b>2. Post: Tooling unlock — the debugger told me the write failed. The write had not failed.</b>
Every embedded engineer eventually hits a bug where the debugger itself is wrong. This week someone wrote up exactly why, on hardware a lot of us use.

Debugging a Cortex-M33 nRF54LM20, a developer kept seeing GDB report stale register values after writes that had actually succeeded. The cause: caching layers inside J-Link and GDB itself, silently serving old data instead of a fresh read. The fix was either explicit ReadMemAP commands with the right access-port and address parameters, or disabling J-Link's cache outright.

The insight that generalizes: your debugger is software, with its own state and its own bugs, sitting between you and the hardware. When your mental model and the readout disagree and the fix doesn't explain it, the tool is a suspect before your logic is.

Has a caching layer ever cost you an afternoon of debugging the wrong thing?

<b>3. Post: Price-floor shift — Google just gave away an NPU design, no royalty attached</b>

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — "design your product to share its own data" is now a real requirement, not a future one</b>
As of September 12, this stopped being a compliance slide and became an engineering constraint.

The EU Data Act's Article 3(1) requires any connected product placed on the EU market to be designed — by default — so the data it generates is easily and securely accessible to the user. That's not a paperwork checkbox; it's a requirement that touches your API design, your local storage layer, and how you think about who "owns" the sensor data flowing through your firmware.

Most engineers meet regulations like this only once a compliance team hands down a finished checklist, long after the architecture is locked in. This one is live now, while EU embedded teams are still deciding how to implement it — which means the engineers thinking about it early are the ones who'll shape what "compliant by design" actually looks like in practice.
