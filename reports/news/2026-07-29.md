<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Researchers disclosed on July 28 that more than 24,000 internet-exposed server Baseboard Management Controllers (BMCs) are leaking authentication material through CVE-2013-4786, a 20-plus-year-old weakness in the IPMI 2.0 RMCP+ protocol: the BMC hands an attacker a valid password hash before any authentication succeeds, letting it be cracked offline with GPU rigs. For roughly a third of the exposed units — mostly Supermicro boards using the default "ADMIN" username — the crackable password turned out to be the ten-character uppercase string printed on the chassis sticker itself. Once cracked, IPMI access gives full out-of-band control: power cycling, virtual media mounting, and firmware flashing, independent of whatever OS or hypervisor is running. The US accounts for 39% of the exposed population. Why it matters: this is the server-room instance of a very embedded problem — a firmware-level management protocol shipped a authentication design flaw two decades ago, vendors printed factory credentials on a label instead of forcing rotation, and neither has meaningfully changed since; the same pattern (management interface, default creds, "it's on an internal network anyway") shows up constantly in IoT and industrial firmware design.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the password was on a sticker the whole time</b>
24,000-plus servers. A vulnerability from 2013. And for a third of them, the "crackable" password was just the ten characters printed on the chassis.

Researchers disclosed this week that server Baseboard Management Controllers are leaking valid authentication hashes to anyone who asks, before login, via a flaw in the IPMI 2.0 protocol (CVE-2013-4786). Once cracked offline, that hash gives full out-of-band control: power cycling, mounting virtual media, flashing firmware — independent of whatever OS is running.

The part worth sitting with: this isn't a zero-day. It's a thirteen-year-old, publicly known design flaw in a firmware management protocol, paired with the same "print the default password on a label" decision embedded vendors have made for two decades. The datacenter and the IoT device have the same disease.

If your product ships a management interface, would it survive someone just... asking for the credential?

<b>2. Post: Capability unlock — NPUs are graduating from vendor blob to kernel driver</b>
Every NPU today ships with its own black-box firmware blob and a compiler toolchain nobody outside the vendor fully understands. That's starting to crack.

An open-source kernel driver now talks to Arm's Ethos-U65/U85 NPU directly from the main CPU through Linux's standard DRM/accel subsystem — no firmware running on a companion Cortex-M core required. A matching open Mesa/Teflon userspace driver is already tested on i.MX93 hardware.

Why this matters more than it sounds: mainline kernel support means the NPU stops being a fenced-off vendor feature and starts being a normal Linux device — inspectable, debuggable, and usable without the SDK lock-in that's made edge-AI hardware notoriously hard to compare or swap.

<b>4. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — starting a circuit on almost no power at all</b>
275 millivolts. 1.5 microwatts. That's the entire input budget needed to cold-start a chip with zero stored energy.

e-peas' new AEM15820 energy-harvesting PMIC combines low- and high-power boost converters with an MPPT stage and a cold-start circuit that wakes the device from that tiny input — aimed at self-charging wearables, e-readers, and remote sensor nodes that today need two or three separate PMICs stitched together to do the same job.

The interesting design constraint isn't the steady-state efficiency — it's the cold start. Getting anything to boot from microwatts, with no battery to fall back on, means every downstream regulator and reference has to work at a fraction of its normal operating point before the system can even measure how much power it has.

Where's the real bottleneck in your last low-power design — steady-state efficiency, or getting it to wake up in the first place?

<b>5. Post: Integration leap — one chip now does compute, networking, and vision I/O</b>
Dual-core RISC-V. Wi-Fi 6. Bluetooth 5.4. Gigabit Ethernet. A camera interface and a 24-bit parallel LCD port. One SoC, now shipping.

Espressif's ESP32-S31 entered mass production this week: a 320 MHz dual-core RISC-V part (one core with a 128-bit SIMD datapath), 512 KB SRAM, 250 MHz DDR PSRAM, and a full wireless stack plus a 1000 Mbps Ethernet MAC and HMI-grade display/camera interfaces, aimed at smart hubs, edge-AI nodes, and industrial gear.

What stands out is how many previously-separate BOM lines this collapses: a networking SoC, a display controller, and a camera bridge used to be three parts and three sets of firmware. Folding them into one chip changes the calculus on board area and firmware complexity for AIoT designs, not just cost.

If you're speccing a new vision-capable IoT node, does this kind of integration change your BOM more than the raw compute numbers do?
