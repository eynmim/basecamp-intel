<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Security researchers disclosed that cellular IoT modems can be attacked directly through the SIM slot: the "Proactive SIM" feature, which lets a SIM card push AT commands to a device's modem, turns out to be exploitable for code execution. On an Autel EV charger built around a Quectel EC25-AFX module, the modem's atfwd_daemon passes SIM-issued text through an unsafe format string into a shell call, and a character blocklist meant to stop shell escapes failed to catch a newline — giving a malicious or cloned SIM full code execution on the modem's application processor. The bug is believed to affect every module in Quectel's EC25/EG25/RM52xN families and possibly other Quectel modules built on Qualcomm basebands, tracked as CVE-2026-57550 / GSMA CVD-2026-0122 — hardware embedded in EV chargers, payment terminals, cars, and general cellular IoT. Disclosed to Qualcomm, Quectel, Google, Oppo, and Semtech in March and to GSMA in May, none of the five vendors had a public advisory live as of August 11, and Quectel's advisory portal sits behind a login wall with no public firmware channel. Why it matters: if your design treats the SIM slot as a passive, dumb component, this proves the cellular modem is itself an attacker-reachable surface — reachable with nothing more than a malicious SIM card.

<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — your SIM card might be attacking your modem, not the other way around</b>
The SIM card in your cellular module isn't a dumb data pipe. It can run code on your modem.

Researchers found that "Proactive SIM," a feature letting a SIM push AT commands to a device's modem, is exploitable for command injection. On an Autel EV charger's Quectel EC25-AFX module, the modem's atfwd_daemon passes SIM-issued text into a shell call through an unsafe format string — and a character blocklist meant to catch shell escapes missed a newline. Full code execution, from a malicious SIM.

The bug likely hits every module in Quectel's EC25/EG25/RM52xN families — hardware inside EV chargers, payment terminals, cars, and countless cellular IoT designs (CVE-2026-57550). Five months after disclosure, none of the five notified vendors has a public advisory live, and Quectel doesn't ship firmware updates publicly — so if you integrated one of these modules, there's no clean way yet to confirm you're patched.

If your threat model treats the SIM slot as passive, this is the reminder it's attacker-reachable input.

<b>3. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — 1.1 microamps of sleep current, on a chip that also measures sub-meter Bluetooth distance</b>
Precision ranging used to mean trading away battery life. Not anymore.

Silicon Labs' new BG2B pairs an 80MHz Cortex-M33 with a 1.1µA RAM-retention sleep current — the company's lowest yet — while being its first part to implement the complete Bluetooth Channel Sounding feature set (Mode 3, NADM, Inline PCT), the same spec Apple and Google use for precision-finding features.

Channel Sounding needs enough RF and timing precision to measure sub-meter distance between two radios, which is normally a power-hungry capability. Landing it on a coin-cell-class part alongside a 1.1µA sleep state says a lot about how far low-power BLE silicon has come: precision ranging is no longer something you have to trade battery life for.

If proximity or ranging ever comes up in a BLE project, this spec is worth reading before you touch the silicon.

<b>4. Post: Regulatory shift — in 30 days, "we'll report it eventually" stops being valid in the EU</b>
September 11, 2026 is when "we'll get to vulnerability reporting later" quietly becomes non-compliant.

That's when the EU Cyber Resilience Act's Article 14 obligation goes live: any manufacturer shipping a product with digital elements into the EU must report actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours, deliver full notification within 72 hours, and file a final report within 14 days of a fix, through ENISA's reporting platform.

This lands more than a year before the CRA's main compliance obligations in December 2027 — meaning the incident-response clock starts running long before most teams finish building the rest of their compliance program. If you ship anything connected into the EU, that 24-hour clock is already inside your current sprint planning horizon, whether or not your compliance roadmap says so.

Does your team already have an owner and a runbook for a 24-hour vulnerability disclosure, or is that still undefined?

<b>5. Post: Tooling unlock — profiling an on-device AI model just became a one-line pip install</b>
Profiling an on-device AI model used to mean hand-instrumenting your own build. Now it's one command.

Ambiq shipped heliaPROFILER, an open-source Python package that automates AI-model profiling end-to-end on its Apollo SoCs — build through execution to report — producing layer-level runtime and memory breakdowns, with optional real-time power measurement, and output compatible with Google's Model Explorer.

The layer-level-plus-power combination is the interesting part: most TinyML profiling tools give you timing or power, rarely both tied to the same layer. Seeing exactly which layer is burning your power budget, not just your latency budget, changes how you'd actually optimize a battery-powered inference pipeline instead of guessing which layer to prune.

Worth trying against your own on-device model even off Ambiq silicon — the profiling approach transfers, even if the tool doesn't.
