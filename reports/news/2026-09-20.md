<b>1. Post: Security wake-up — the same Linux kernel bug just showed up in a federal deadline and a public exploit</b>
Two different disclosures, one kernel, and now agencies have 72 hours instead of a patch cycle.

CISA added three actively-exploited Linux kernel CVEs to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog this week — a CVSS 9.8 TLS receive-path flaw, an 8.8 netfilter/ebtables out-of-bounds write, and a 7.8 AF_ALG race condition — with a hard remediation deadline under Binding Operational Directive 26-04. <b>2. Post: Integration leap — five wireless protocols and two instruction sets on one small die</b>
Bluetooth LE, Channel Sounding, Thread, Zigbee and Matter used to mean picking a chip per protocol. Nordic just put all five on one.

The new nRF54LC10A pairs a 128MHz Arm Cortex-M33 core with a RISC-V coprocessor on a 22nm process, in a package as small as 1.9×2.3mm, with sleep current down to 0.5µA. It's sampling now, with production targeted for Q2 2027.

The interesting design choice isn't the protocol list, it's the RISC-V coprocessor riding alongside the Cortex-M33 — offloading radio timing or protocol-stack work to a second core changes the calculus for how much of your application logic you can leave on the main core without touching real-time guarantees.

If your next design needs BLE plus a mesh protocol, would you rather manage that tradeoff in software on one core, or let silicon split it for you?

<b>3. Post: Capability unlock — 34% more ESP32 performance from a firmware default, not new silicon</b>
Every ESP32-S3 board you already own just got faster, and you don't have to buy anything.

ESPHome 2026.4.0 changes the default CPU clock on ESP32, S2, S3 and C5 chips from 160MHz to 240MHz, cutting API-encryption handshake time from 90ms to 64ms and speeding up protobuf and noise-encryption paths by roughly a third. The original ESP32 also picks up an extra 40kB of usable instruction memory. The framework checks bootloader compatibility automatically before switching it on.

The insight that's easy to miss: the silicon could always do this. What changed is a software default, which means a meaningful chunk of "better performance" work on constrained MCUs is really about auditing your own default configs, not waiting on next-gen hardware.

Worth checking: is your own ESP32 project still running at a default clock speed you never revisited?

<b>4. Post: Physics-meets-architecture — Zigbee just traded 2.4GHz's crowding for sub-GHz's reach</b>
More range and better wall penetration were never a 2.4GHz problem you could fix with more antennas. They're a frequency problem.

The Connectivity Standards Alliance opened its certification program for "Suzi," Zigbee's new sub-GHz extension, covering 800MHz in Europe and 900MHz in North America alongside the existing 2.4GHz band. It follows last year's Zigbee 4.0 release and gives device makers a formal interoperability and security certification path for sub-GHz hardware.

The physics is the whole story here: lower frequency means longer wavelength, which means better diffraction around walls and furniture — exactly the indoor-mesh weak spot 2.4GHz-only Zigbee networks have always had, at the cost of the bandwidth you get up at 2.4GHz.

If your last mesh deployment fought dead zones indoors, was it ever really a protocol problem, or a frequency-band problem?

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — the EU now runs two overlapping cybersecurity rulebooks for connected products</b>
If you thought the Cyber Resilience Act replaced the old rules the moment it landed, it didn't — for over a year, you're compliant with both or neither.

Since September 2026, the Radio Equipment Directive's cybersecurity provisions and the Cyber Resilience Act's early vulnerability-reporting obligations are both legally in force for connected products sold into the EU. RED's cybersecurity piece only gets repealed and folded into the CRA once the CRA's full product-security regime takes effect in December 2027.

The part that catches people out: this isn't a clean handoff on a fixed date. It's a 15-month window where a wireless embedded product needs to satisfy two separate legal frameworks simultaneously, and "I'll deal with it when RED gets replaced" isn't actually a valid compliance strategy during that overlap.

If you're building anything with a radio that might ship into the EU, does your compliance checklist currently cover both regimes, or just the one you heard about first?
