<b>═ 🔥 TOP STORY ═</b>
Researchers at Northwestern University published EITWatch, a fully open-source smartwatch that reads hand gestures via planar electrical impedance tomography — built entirely on a Seeed Studio XIAO ESP32-S3. Instead of the usual circumferential electrode band other wrist-EIT systems use, EITWatch puts all eight electrodes on the back of a standard 40mm watch case, sequencing impedance measurements across them at 48Hz to detect the muscle and tendon movement that produces a hand gesture, with the analog front end riding on a small PCB extension. Every design file — Altium PCB layout, Gerbers, BoM (CERN-OHL-P-2.0) and the ESP32-S3 C firmware (Apache-2.0) — is public. Why it matters: it's a complete, reproducible biosensing pipeline (analog front end, real-time signal processing, gesture classification) running entirely on the exact SoC most of us already use for Wi-Fi/BLE work, on hardware costing a few dollars — a genuinely different application domain to add to an ESP32-S3 portfolio beyond IoT connectivity.

<b>1. Post: Capability unlock — a smartwatch just learned to read your hand gestures through your skin's electrical resistance</b>
Eight electrodes on the back of a watch case, 48 measurements a second, and no camera or IMU anywhere in the design.

Researchers at Northwestern built EITWatch, an open-source smartwatch that detects hand gestures using electrical impedance tomography — reading the impedance changes that muscle and tendon movement cause beneath the skin. It runs entirely on a Seeed XIAO ESP32-S3, with all eight sensing electrodes on the back of a standard 40mm case instead of the usual circumferential band other wrist-EIT designs use.

The interesting part is the electrode placement choice: putting everything on the case back instead of wrapping the wrist trades some signal richness for a form factor that actually fits a normal watch. That's the real engineering trade EIT-based wearables have to make, and this is a fully open (PCB files, BoM, firmware) reference for how one team solved it.

<b>2. Post: Security wake-up — the chip that's supposed to prove your device is trustworthy can now lie about it</b>
A TPM's entire job is to be the one component you can trust unconditionally. AMD's firmware TPM just proved that assumption has a hole in it.

CVE-2026-6726 and CVE-2026-6727 stem from a use-after-free in the shared TCG reference code AMD's fTPM builds on: discarded key objects aren't properly invalidated, so an attacker can present a forged key at the same memory address as a genuine one. The impact isn't data theft — it's forged attestation, meaning a compromised machine can present fake TPM credentials and pass the exact zero-trust health checks organizations rely on to keep compromised devices off the network.

The bug reaches Ryzen Embedded parts too, the family used in industrial and embedded x86 designs — attestation isn't just a laptop-security story, it's an embedded one.

If your design relies on TPM attestation as a trust anchor, is that assumption still holding up under scrutiny?

<b>4. Post: Tooling unlock — ST just made SEGGER's debug probe a first-class citizen in its own toolchain</b>
For years, using a SEGGER J-Link with STMicro's official programming tool meant working around the tool, not with it. That's over.

STM32CubeProgrammer 2.23 ships with native SEGGER J-Link support and SWD Multidrop capability, alongside expanded coverage for the STM32N6, STM32C0, STM32WL3, and the new STM32H7R/S families.

The SWD Multidrop piece is the one worth pausing on: it lets a single J-Link address multiple STM32 targets on the same SWD bus without swapping probes or connections — genuinely useful the moment a board has more than one MCU on it, which is increasingly common in sensor-fusion or gateway designs.

If you've been keeping a separate ST-Link around just for CubeProgrammer compatibility, that's one less cable to carry.

<b>5. Post: Regulatory shift — Europe just admitted its own AI law isn't ready for the deadline it set</b>
Imagine writing a law, then discovering the technical standard it depends on won't be finished in time — and pushing the deadline instead of the standard.

That's what happened with the EU AI Act's Digital Omnibus, signed July 8: compliance for AI embedded in regulated products (medical devices, machinery, vehicles) now has until August 2028, and stand-alone high-risk systems until December 2027 — both delayed because European standardisation bodies are behind on the harmonized technical standards the obligations were supposed to be assessed against.

What didn't move: Article 50 transparency rules — AI-generated content marking, chatbot disclosure, deepfake labeling — took effect August 2, 2026, on the original schedule, regardless of the Omnibus. The lesson for anyone building embedded AI features: transparency obligations arrive well before the "real" compliance deadlines do, so they're the ones to plan around first.
